The incidental liver lesion

Up to 15% of patients have an incidental liver lesion on routine non-surveillance imaging, and up to 52% of people without cancer have a benign liver lesion at autopsy. Most solid lesions in a patient with no risk factors will be benign — haemangioma, adenoma or FNH.

First — which of these four is this?

Cirrhosis or chronic liver disease present

Stop — this is not a benign-lesion problem

Any lesion in a cirrhotic liver is HCC until proven otherwise. Follow the AASLD HCC guidance and LI-RADS categorisation. Benign lesions rarely form de novo in a cirrhotic liver, and 97% of lesions with characteristic LI-RADS features need no biopsy.

History of malignancy

Assume metastasis until characterised

Follow the guidance for that primary tumour, or discuss at a multidisciplinary meeting. A new lesion in a patient under surveillance for a known cancer is far more likely metastatic than a newly appeared benign lesion. MRI with hepatobiliary contrast plus diffusion-weighted imaging is the recommended modality for suspected metastases.

Under 40, unremarkable history, examination and bloods

MRI with hepatobiliary contrast

The one group where a hepatobiliary agent is clearly first choice, because the practical question is FNH versus adenoma — and their management differs completely. FNH is hyperintense on the hepatobiliary phase; most other lesions are hypointense.

Everyone else

Multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging

Triple-phase contrast-enhanced CT, or dynamic MRI with either hepatobiliary or extracellular contrast. Expert opinion favours extracellular agents first line outside the FNH question, because hepatobiliary agents can cause transient dyspnoea and give less satisfactory dynamic phases.

The workup

History

  • Previous cancer of any kind
  • Constitutional symptoms — weight loss, anorexia, fever, night sweats
  • Medications: oral contraceptives, anabolic steroids, hormone therapy
  • Foreign travel
  • Risk factors for chronic liver disease — viral hepatitis, transfusions, tattoos, intravenous drug use, alcohol
  • Features of the metabolic syndrome — obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease

Bloods

  • Liver enzymes
  • Viral hepatitis panel
  • Tumour markers where the differential warrants them
  • Alkaline phosphatase and CRP may be raised with inflammatory adenomas, though not sensitive enough to check routinely

Imaging

  • Multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging with late arterial, portal venous and delayed phases — strong recommendation
  • MRI or CT are statistically equivalent in accuracy, but MRI confers many advantages and avoids ionising radiation in young patients facing years of surveillance
  • CEUS is an alternative with 97.8% sensitivity and 83.9% specificity for benign lesion characterisation
  • A routine single portal-venous-phase abdominal CT cannot characterise a lesion — it detects one

Two lesions that need nothing further

Characteristic on ultrasound or single-phase CT — stop here
  • Haemangioma — a small echogenic avascular lesion under 2 cm with well-defined borders, in a normal liver, with no risk factors for liver disease or malignancyAnything outside that description needs multiphasic imaging
  • Uncomplicated simple cyst — anechoic, through transmission, smooth marginsAny high-risk feature moves it to CT or MRI
Extracellular versus hepatobiliary contrast. Hepatobiliary agents are partially excreted by the biliary system, so lesions with biliary pathology — FNH — are hyperintense on the hepatobiliary phase, while most other lesions are hypointense. That makes them the clear choice in a young patient with no malignancy risk factors, where the question is FNH versus adenoma. Outside that scenario there is no consensus, and expert opinion favours extracellular agents because hepatobiliary agents cause transient dyspnoea and give less adequate dynamic phases.
When a diagnosis cannot be made: multidisciplinary discussion and consideration of core biopsy. If the lesion cannot be characterised and is not amenable to biopsy, surveillance imaging every 3–6 months to detect change in size, appearance or the development of new lesions. Inadequately characterised or atypical lesions should be reviewed at a multidisciplinary liver tumour board — this appears as a key concept in the guideline and recurs throughout it.
Haemangioma the commonest benign solid lesion — up to 20% of the population, F:M 4:1 No follow-up once confirmed · Do not biopsy, do not resect
Diagnosis
Ultrasound alone is enough for a small echogenic avascular lesion under 2 cm with well-defined borders, in a patient with a normal liver and no risk factors for liver disease or malignancy. Anything that does not meet that description needs multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging.MRI has the best performance — sensitivity 92–100%, specificity 86–99%. Classic features: T2 "lightbulb bright", peripheral nodular enhancement with progressive centripetal fill-in following the blood pool.
Management
None. Even large cavernous haemangiomas over 10 cm, if asymptomatic, need neither resection nor follow-up. Resection is indicated only for complications: rupture, intralesional haemorrhage, consumptive coagulopathy (Kasabach–Merritt), or compression of an adjacent organ or vessel — all rare.
Follow-up
No follow-up imaging once the diagnosis is confirmed. Two exceptions:
• Cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B meeting HCC surveillance criteria — continue imaging every 3–6 months for at least 1 year (strong recommendation), because early HCC can mimic a haemangioma.
• Nothing else. Pregnancy does not require monitoring, even with large cavernous lesions.
Worth knowingAvoid biopsy because of bleeding risk, though the measured risk with small needles is only 0.15%. Three types: cavernous (classic), capillary or "flash-filling" (under 1 cm, rapid homogeneous arterial enhancement), and sclerosed — the diagnostic trap, which shows thick rim enhancement with non-centripetal progressive fill and capsular retraction, and mimics intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or hypovascular metastasis. Up to 20% of patients with FNH also have a haemangioma.
Focal nodular hyperplasia second commonest solid lesion — 0.3–3%, female preponderance No follow-up once confirmed · Do not stop the pill
Diagnosis
Multiphase MRI with a hepatobiliary contrast agent is the modality of choice — over 90% accuracy and nearly 100% specificity. Biopsy is not routinely needed.Well-circumscribed homogeneous arterial hyperenhancement becoming isoenhancing on portal venous and delayed phases; a central scar that is hypo- or isointense without contrast, enhances late, and is T2 hyperintense; and — the decisive feature — hyperintensity on the 20-minute hepatobiliary phase, because FNH contains hepatocyte-specific membrane transporters. Only 2% are hypointense.
Management
Conservative. Not premalignant, and it does not need removing. Even with growth, resection is not required. If resection is being considered for symptoms, counsel the patient explicitly that the symptoms may not improve — FNH rarely causes any. If symptomatic and surgery is not an option, transarterial embolisation with or without bleomycin may reduce size.
Follow-up
No further follow-up once confidently diagnosed on imaging.
Worth knowingDo not routinely stop oral contraceptives — the old belief that FNH is hormone-driven has been disproven, and there is no evidence of change during pregnancy. Men with FNH need no different evaluation, monitoring or treatment.

The important caveat: 11% of inflammatory and 59% of β-catenin mutated adenomas express OATP1B3 and can therefore be hyperintense on the hepatobiliary phase too. Any lesion that is atypical for FNH but hepatobiliary-phase hyperintense must be followed closely rather than dismissed.
Hepatic epithelioid haemangioendothelioma HEHE — rare vascular tumour of intermediate grade Resect whenever possible · Transplant if unresectable
Diagnosis
Incidence around 1–2 per million; diagnosed incidentally in 25%. Look for capsular retraction, calcification and multifocality, and the "lollipop sign" — tapering of a portal or hepatic vein branch as it approaches the lesion, occluded like a stick in the sweet. If imaging is highly suspicious and resection is planned, needle biopsy is not necessarily required first.
Management
Resect whenever possible. If resection is not feasible, liver transplantation offers the best survival — even in the presence of extrahepatic disease, which is unusual among liver tumours and worth remembering. Stage with whole-body contrast-enhanced CT or MRI; PET is only mildly to moderately avid.
Follow-up
Specialist centre. For non-resectable, non-transplantable disease the data are very limited — ablation and stereotactic radiotherapy have shown some response in small series; no systemic therapy can be recommended from published evidence.
Worth knowingDistant metastases are present at diagnosis in about 37%, yet median survival is still around 182 months. Poor prognostic features: lung or multiorgan involvement, ascites with serosal metastasis, age over 55, male sex.
Fibrolamellar HCC FLHCC — young patients, non-cirrhotic liver Surgical resection · Biopsy to confirm
Diagnosis
Biopsy should be performed to confirm the diagnosis — but molecular analysis of the biopsy is not beneficial for guiding systemic therapy.
Management
Surgical resection is the treatment of choice. Where disease is limited to the liver but unresectable, transplant may be considered case by case.
Follow-up
Specialist oncology follow-up.
Worth knowingNeoadjuvant and adjuvant systemic therapy are not recommended outside a clinical trial.
Hepatic angiosarcoma rare, aggressive — under 1% of primary hepatic malignancy Resect if feasible · Refer to a sarcoma centre
Diagnosis
Varied appearance — multiple masses or a single heterogeneous one. Hypoenhancing on all postcontrast phases unless there is haemorrhage. Around 80% are over 50 years old, slight male predominance. The only known risk factors are thorium dioxide, vinyl chloride, arsenic and radiation.
Management
Surgical resection whenever feasible. Resection prolongs survival, though recurrence is frequent.
Follow-up
Sarcoma specialty centre.
Worth knowingOne-year survival is around 12.8%. Adjuvant radiation and chemotherapy data are mixed and not recommended.
Metastatic disease the commonest malignant lesion in a non-cirrhotic liver MRI with HBP contrast + DWI · Work up the primary
Diagnosis
MRI with a hepatocyte-specific contrast agent combined with diffusion-weighted imaging is the recommended modality. Per-lesion sensitivity for precontrast, combined dynamic and delayed hepatobiliary imaging is 77.8%, 88.1% and 95.1% respectively. Contrast-enhanced CT reaches about 74%.
Management
Directed by the primary. In a patient with a known extrahepatic primary, a new lesion found on staging is almost always a metastasis and does not need dedicated liver characterisation — proceed to staging and biopsy. In a patient with no known primary but suspicious imaging, work up to find it.
Follow-up
Per the primary tumour guidance.
Worth knowingMetastatic colorectal cancer is a special case: patients with unresectable liver-limited metastases meeting molecular criteria who have responded to chemotherapy for at least 6 months may be considered for liver transplantation at an experienced centre. Refer patients with HCC, cholangiocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumours or metastatic colon cancer to a transplant centre early in their course.

Hepatocellular adenoma

The lesion that generates most of the decisions. Benign, but with a risk of haemorrhage up to 15% and malignant transformation up to 5% — and both risks are concentrated in identifiable subgroups.

Risk factors: oral contraceptives, anabolic steroids, obesity and the metabolic syndrome, glycogen storage disease (types Ia and III especially), polycystic ovarian syndrome, and exogenous hormonal therapy in men, women and transgender individuals. Adenomas in glycogen storage disease present early — often in the second or third decade — with a 2:1 male predominance.

Management by group

Every patient with an adenoma

Stop oral contraceptives and hormone-impregnated intrauterine devices — strong recommendation. Stop anabolic steroids. Encourage weight loss if overweight or obese; regression follows hormone withdrawal and metabolic control in many cases.

Men — any size, any subtype

Surgical resection regardless of lesion size. Adenomas in men are commonly β-catenin mutated and carry roughly a tenfold higher risk of malignant transformation than in women. Biopsy is not advised before surgery, but should be done if the patient is not a surgical candidate and will need ablation or embolisation instead.

Women, lesion under 5 cm

Stop exogenous hormones, advise weight loss. Surveillance with contrast-enhanced imaging every 6 months for 2 years, then annually. Routine biopsy for subtyping is not recommended in this group. Consider biopsy only if there is concern for β-catenin mutation — if positive, resect.

Women, lesion 5 cm or larger

Modify risk factors and observe for 6–12 months. Repeat contrast-enhanced MRI at 6 months. If the lesion regresses below 5 cm, continue surveillance. If it remains 5 cm or larger, resect — or embolise if not a surgical candidate.

Any lesion growing by 20% or more

Resection or definitive treatment. The 20% threshold is extrapolated from RECIST criteria. Any imaging feature suggesting malignancy warrants immediate treatment without a period of observation.

Imaging features concerning for malignant transformation

Treat as hepatocellular carcinoma — surgical resection, locoregional therapy, or transplantation. Warning signs: rapid increase in size, new intralesional lipid, and new washout on portal venous or delayed phases. AFP is unreliable here.

Unable to undergo surgery

Embolisation or ablation as alternatives. Embolisation carries a risk of incomplete eradication and possible need for retreatment, so it may serve as a bridge. Continue surveillance every 6–12 months after resection, embolisation or ablation.

Ruptured adenoma

Haemodynamic stabilisation first, then transarterial embolisation and/or surgical resection. Do not take an unstable patient straight to theatre. Established risk factors for rupture: size 5 cm or more, growth, and exophytic location. Residual adenoma after haemorrhage should be resected — laparoscopically if possible — because recurrent haemorrhage and growth remain a risk.

Pregnancy

Repeat ultrasound every 6–12 weeks. Lesions under 5 cm, non-exophytic, without growth can be monitored safely and do not affect the mode of delivery. Growth beyond 6.5 cm or high-risk features for rupture warrant treatment: resection early in pregnancy, or embolisation before 26 weeks. Emergent surgery for rupture in the third trimester. Greatest risk is the third trimester. Before planned conception, consider treating lesions under 5 cm if high risk, and treat those 5 cm or larger.

Adenomatosis — 10 or more lesions

A distinct entity, usually on a background of steatosis or glycogen storage disease, commonly HNF-1α subtype. Bleeding risk up to 46% and malignancy in up to 7%. Unilobar disease: resection. Bilobar: focus on the largest or dominant lesion, or any lesion raising suspicion. Monitor at least annually. If glycogen storage disease is suspected, arrange genetic testing.

Transplantation

For patients meeting OPTN policy — particularly glycogen storage disease, unresectable β-catenin positive adenoma, or unresectable disease with complications of haemorrhage or malignant transformation.

Molecular subtypes

MRI can subtype inflammatory and HNF-1α adenomas reliably. It cannot subtype β-catenin, sonic hedgehog or unclassified adenomas — which is precisely where the risk sits.

Inflammatory ~35–45%
Risk factors: Excess oestrogen, obesity, hepatic steatosis, heavy alcohol, glycogen storage disease
Complications: Haemorrhage low · malignancy moderate

Complications: Haemorrhage low · malignancy low

Biopsy when the appearance is uncharacteristic, or when imaging features change in a way that raises concern for malignant transformation. If biopsy shows a β-catenin exon 3 mutation, or transformation to HCC, proceed to resection. If it confirms HNF-1α, inflammatory or another low-risk profile, continue surveillance. Routine biopsy for subtyping is not advised for lesions under 5 cm in women until prospective data support it.
A pathology caveat. Distinguishing β-catenin activated adenoma from well-differentiated HCC is difficult on histology as well as imaging. The three-marker panel of glypican-3, heat shock protein 70 and glutamine synthetase helps. Where pathology is indeterminate, the terms "atypical hepatocellular neoplasm" and "hepatocellular neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential" are used, and management is decided at the tumour board.
Simple hepatic cyst thin-walled, bile duct epithelium, isotonic fluid No surveillance at any size · Treat only if symptomatic
Diagnosis
Ultrasound is diagnostic with 90% sensitivity and specificity: homogeneously anechoic, through transmission, smooth margins. On CT, no internal architecture, attenuation under 20 Hounsfield units, no postcontrast enhancement. On MRI, T1 hypointense, T2 hyperintense, no enhancement, with decreasing intensity on high b-value diffusion. Up to 2.5–5% of simple cysts contain up to two septa and are still simple.
Management
Expectant management regardless of size — strong recommendation. No routine surveillance and no intervention for asymptomatic cysts, however large.

If symptomatic: surgical cyst fenestration (deroofing) or aspiration with sclerotherapy. Surgery has the lowest recurrence rate and allows histology. Aspiration alone is not recommended as definitive treatment — the recurrence rate is high, though it is useful to confirm the cyst is the cause of the symptoms.
Follow-up
None. After sclerotherapy, do not repeat intervention within 6 months — it can take that long to see maximum benefit — and no postintervention imaging is needed.
Worth knowingHigh-risk features on ultrasound mandate CT or MRI (strong recommendation): septations, fenestrations, calcifications, mural thickening or nodularity, heterogeneity, or daughter cysts. These raise the possibility of infection, abscess, cystic metastasis, hydatid disease or mucinous cystic neoplasm.

Serum CA 19-9 is raised in up to 50% of patients with simple cysts and does not help distinguish benign from malignant; cyst fluid CA 19-9 is equally unhelpful.
Complex hepatic cyst anything not meeting simple criteria Multidisciplinary discussion · Surveillance 6–12 months
Diagnosis
Defined by what it is not. Any septation beyond two, fenestration, calcification, mural thickening or nodularity, heterogeneity or daughter cyst moves the lesion into this category.
Management
Discussion at a multidisciplinary tumour board, whatever the size and even if asymptomatic.
Follow-up
Consider surveillance imaging at 6–12 months.
Worth knowingThe differential runs from entirely benign to frankly malignant: infected cyst, pyogenic abscess, cystic metastasis, hydatid cyst, and mucinous cystic neoplasm.
Polycystic liver disease PCLD — at least 10 cysts No surveillance — no malignant potential · Somatostatin analogues
Diagnosis
At least 10 hepatic cysts with characteristics similar to simple cysts. Part of the ciliopathy spectrum. Patients with ADPKD should have an abdominal ultrasound to screen for PCLD; patients with apparently isolated PCLD should have a kidney ultrasound to exclude ADPKD. Genetic testing plays little role — the six known PCLD genes account for only 30–45% of cases — though it may help in young patients with few renal cysts.
Management
Stop exogenous oestrogen — oestrogen-containing contraceptives worsen PCLD, adding up to 15.5% higher height-adjusted liver volume per decade of use.

For numerous small-to-medium cysts not amenable to resection, fenestration or sclerotherapy — and for symptomatic ADPKD with concurrent PCLD — somatostatin analogues are a strong recommendation with moderate evidence. Lanreotide LAR 120 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks reduced height-adjusted liver volume by nearly 6%, with most of the effect in the first 6 months.

Aspiration with sclerotherapy suits large dominant cysts. Surgical fenestration reduces volume and gives immediate relief but recurs in about 30%. Resection where fenestration will not work.
Follow-up
Imaging surveillance is not recommended in asymptomatic patients — there is no malignant potential.
Worth knowingLiver transplantation, with or without simultaneous kidney transplantation, is the only curative option and has excellent long-term survival. Consider it for marked synthetic dysfunction, malnutrition, or significant impairment of quality of life. MELD exception points are available for hepatic decompensation, concurrent haemodialysis, GFR under 20, previous kidney transplant, documented moderate-to-severe protein-calorie malnutrition, or severe sarcopenia. mTOR inhibitors and ursodiol have not shown benefit.
Mucinous cystic neoplasm MCN-L — formerly biliary cystadenoma Complete surgical resection · Never aspirate to diagnose
Diagnosis
Under 5% of cystic liver lesions, strong female predominance (4:1), fifth to sixth decade, 76% in the left lobe with a predilection for segment IV. Does not communicate with the biliary tree — which is what separates it from IPNB.Septations are 95% sensitive. Septations arising directly from the cyst wall are 100% specific; mural calcifications are 90% specific; upstream biliary dilatation is highly specific. Enhancement of septations and mural nodularity is nearly 100% sensitive on MRI.
Management
Complete surgical resection — the gold standard, laparoscopic or open. Thick septations, fenestrations, nodularity, calcifications or mixed solid and cystic components require prompt evaluation for resection.

Aspiration, sclerosis, partial resection and fenestration are all contraindicated — the recurrence rate is 81%.
Follow-up
For patients who are not surgical candidates, surveillance imaging should be implemented, although no specific interval can be recommended. Any change suggesting malignant degeneration goes to the tumour board.
Worth knowingFluid aspiration and biopsy are not recommended to distinguish benign from malignant — the sensitivity is too low. Malignant transformation risk is around 10% under current WHO criteria, down from the historical 20–30%.
Biliary hamartomas & peribiliary cysts von Meyenburg complexes Benign malformations · No surveillance
Diagnosis
Hamartomas: multiple cystic lesions that do not communicate with the biliary tree, usually under 1.5 cm, often peripheral, anywhere in the liver. They do not affect liver function tests.
Peribiliary cysts: perihilar, under 1 cm, seen on both sides of the bile ducts as a "string of pearls" around the hilar portal veins — commonly in chronic liver disease or portal hypertension.
Management
None.
Follow-up
No surveillance imaging required.
Worth knowingMalignant transformation to intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or HCC is rare and confined to case reports, mostly in patients with underlying liver disease, congenital hepatic fibrosis or Caroli disease.
Intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct IPNB — the biliary analogue of pancreatic IPMN Premalignant · Surveillance even after resection
Diagnosis
A precursor to cholangiocarcinoma, but with a much higher rate of malignant transformation than pancreatic IPMN — 40–80% harbour malignancy. Median age 60–66, male-to-female 2:1. Presents with recurrent abdominal pain, cholangitis and jaundice. Risk factors: hepatolithiasis, clonorchiasis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, choledochal cysts, familial adenomatous polyposis and Gardner syndrome.
Management
Surgical resection of the bile duct, with or without associated hepatectomy depending on size, extent and invasiveness.
Follow-up
Continued surveillance imaging is recommended even after surgical resection, because of the high rate of undetected lesions remote from the main tumour — which are a source of recurrence.
Worth knowingDistinguished from MCN-L by biliary communication, the presence of intraductal masses and bile duct dilatation as a dominant feature.
Choledochal cysts Todani types I–V · intrahepatic and extrahepatic Management by type · Types I and IV carry malignant risk
Diagnosis
Rare dilatations of the bile ducts, female predominance 4:1, more common in Asian populations. 80% present in the first decade but adult incidence is rising. Thought to arise from reflux of pancreatic enzymes through an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction, which itself increases malignant risk. MRI with MRCP is the best modality — it detects the anomalous junction without instrumenting the biliary tree.The classic triad of pain, jaundice and a palpable mass is rare; abdominal pain alone occurs in 60%.
Management
Type I — complete excision with hepaticoenterostomy
Type II — cyst excision or diverticulectomy
Type III — endoscopic sphincteroplasty or transduodenal excision
Type IV — extrahepatic cyst excision with or without partial hepatectomy and hepaticoenterostomy, or transplantation
Type V (Caroli disease) — resection or transplantation

In types IV and V, where resection is not feasible, liver transplantation should be considered.
Follow-up
Continue surveillance after treatment for types I and IV — the types most associated with malignancy — although a specific interval cannot be recommended. Types II, III and V need no surveillance after definitive treatment.
Worth knowingMalignancy risk overall 7.5–30%, under 1% in young children but 30–40% over the age of 50 — it rises with each decade. Most commonly cholangiocarcinoma (~70%), then gallbladder cancer (23.5%). Type V should be distinguished from Caroli syndrome, which adds congenital hepatic fibrosis and kidney cysts.
Hydatid / echinococcal cyst WHO-IWGE classification CE1–CE5 Antihelminthic before any puncture · Surgery if complicated
Diagnosis
Classified by ultrasound appearance:
CE1 univesicular fluid collection — active
CE2 multivesicular with daughter cysts or septa, honeycomb — active
CE3A detached membrane, the water lily sign — transitional
CE3B daughter cysts in a solid matrix — transitional
CE4 heterogeneous matrix, no daughter cysts, "wool clew" — inactive
CE5 solid matrix with a calcified wall — inactive
Management
CE1 and CE3A: under 5 cm — albendazole alone; over 5 cm — PAIR (puncture, aspiration, injection of a scolicidal agent, reaspiration) plus albendazole.
CE2 and CE3B: catheterisation or surgery plus albendazole.
CE4 and CE5: watch and wait.

Complicated cysts go to surgery — biliary fistula or communication with the biliary tree, multiseptated cysts, rupture or haemorrhage, secondary infection, or percutaneously inaccessible cysts — provided there is no contraindication.
Follow-up
Surveillance if not resectable.
Worth knowingAntihelminthic therapy is indicated before surgery or cyst puncture — to prevent recurrence and secondary seeding, and to reduce cyst pressure. Medical therapy alone is not recommended because it is ineffective, unless PAIR and surgery are both contraindicated.

Quick answers

The questions this guideline settles most decisively.

Does a confirmed haemangioma need follow-up?
AnswerNo — unless there is cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B, where surveillance every 3–6 months for at least a year is a strong recommendation.
Does a confirmed FNH need follow-up?
AnswerNo. And do not stop the oral contraceptive, and do not treat men differently.
Should a large asymptomatic haemangioma be resected?
AnswerNo — not even over 10 cm. Resection is only for complications.
Does an asymptomatic simple cyst need surveillance?
AnswerNo, at any size. Strong recommendation.
Does a man with an adenoma need resection?
AnswerYes, regardless of size — the β-catenin association makes malignant transformation roughly tenfold more likely than in women.
Can I aspirate a cyst to find out if it is a mucinous neoplasm?
AnswerNo. Sensitivity is too low, and partial procedures on an MCN-L carry an 81% recurrence rate. Resect it.
Is CA 19-9 useful in a liver cyst?
AnswerNo. Raised in up to 50% of simple cysts, in serum and in cyst fluid.
Does polycystic liver disease need surveillance imaging?
AnswerNo in asymptomatic patients — there is no malignant potential.

About this page

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Built from the ACG clinical guideline on focal liver lesions. It covers lesions in people without known liver disease — if cirrhosis is present, the AASLD HCC guidance and LI-RADS apply instead.
The recurring instruction in this guideline is to take inadequately characterised or atypical lesions to a multidisciplinary liver tumour board. It appears as a key concept and then again for hepatic adenoma, haemangioma, complex cysts and mucinous cystic neoplasms. Where this page says "discuss", that is the guideline speaking, not hedging.
