Three questions, in order

Almost every periprocedural decision falls out of these three answers. Work through them in this sequence — the procedure comes first, because a low-risk procedure ends the conversation.

1

How much does the procedure bleed?

If it is a low-risk procedure, nothing is stopped. No anticoagulant, no antiplatelet, no exceptions worth the trouble. Most of the interruptions that happen in practice are for procedures that never needed one.

2

How much does the patient clot?

This only matters once you know the procedure is high risk. It decides whether a warfarin patient needs bridging, and whether a P2Y12 inhibitor may be stopped at all.

3

Which drug, and what is the renal function?

Then the hold time follows mechanically. Renal function changes the answer only for dabigatran, and at the extremes for the others.

Procedure bleeding risk

Low risk — change nothing
  • Diagnostic OGD or colonoscopy, including biopsiesBiopsy does not change the risk category
  • Diagnostic flexible sigmoidoscopy ± biopsy
  • ERCP with stent placement or balloon dilatation of a papillary stricture, without sphincterotomy
  • Biliary or pancreatic stenting
  • Device-assisted enteroscopy without polypectomy
  • EUS without sampling or intervention
  • Oesophageal, enteral or colonic stent insertion
  • Capsule endoscopy
High risk — interruption is on the table
  • Polypectomy — any technique, any sizeSee the cold-snare note below
  • EMR and ESDESD carries the highest delayed-bleeding risk of anything on this list
  • AmpullectomyThe one procedure where even aspirin is individualised
  • ERCP with sphincterotomyBalloon sphincteroplasty without sphincterotomy is lower risk
  • EUS with FNA/FNB or any EUS-guided intervention
  • PEG or PEJ placement
  • Oesophageal, enteral or colonic stricture dilatation
  • Variceal band ligation or sclerotherapy
  • Tumour ablation, RFA, APC
  • Pneumatic dilatation for achalasia, POEM
Cold snare, small polyps. Cold snare polypectomy of polyps ≤ 10 mm is the grey zone. A meta-analysis found a pooled delayed bleeding rate of 1.6% when antithrombotics were continued — acceptable for diminutive polyps on aspirin or warfarin, but the same analysis found the risk rises with DOACs and with multiple agents. If you know in advance that only diminutive polyps are expected, continuing is defensible; if you may meet a large or pedunculated lesion, plan as for a high-risk procedure.
Very high risk. ESD, large or piecemeal EMR, and ampullectomy sit above the rest. Meta-analysis data suggest delaying resumption of antithrombotics to about a week after ESD reduces delayed bleeding without a measurable thrombotic cost. Decide the restart plan with the resecting endoscopist, not by protocol.

Thrombotic risk — anticoagulated patients

High thrombotic risk if any of these
  • Prosthetic metal heart valve in the mitral positionAny mitral mechanical valve is high risk
  • Prosthetic metal valve in the aortic position with an additional risk factorAF, prior thromboembolism, LV dysfunction, hypercoagulable state
  • AF with a mechanical valve, or AF with mitral stenosis
  • AF with stroke or TIA within the last 3 months
  • AF with a prior stroke/TIA plus ≥ 3 of: heart failure, hypertension, age > 75, diabetes
  • Venous thromboembolism within the last 3 months
  • Severe thrombophilia — antiphospholipid syndrome, protein C/S or antithrombin deficiency
Everything else — including most non-valvular AF, and VTE more than 3 months old — counts as low thrombotic risk, and does not need bridging.

Thrombotic risk — antiplatelet patients

Do not stop a P2Y12 inhibitor if any of these
  • Drug-eluting coronary stent within 12 monthsNewer-generation stents in high-bleeding-risk patients may allow 1–6 months — ask cardiology
  • Bare-metal coronary stent within 1 month
  • Acute coronary syndrome within 90 days
  • Recent carotid or peripheral arterial stentDiscuss with the implanting service
The practical rule for stents: defer an elective high-risk procedure until the mandatory dual-therapy window has passed — 12 months after a drug-eluting stent, 1 month after a bare-metal stent, and beyond 90 days after an ACS. Newer-generation drug-eluting stents allow shorter windows in patients at high bleeding risk, but that call belongs to cardiology, in writing, not to an assumption.

The patient the 2021 guideline forgot

Anticoagulant monotherapy in someone with a prior coronary stent. The BSG/ESGE 2021 guideline is silent on this group. BSG issued a formal addendum on 3 June 2024 after at least two fatal myocardial infarctions from stent thrombosis in patients whose anticoagulant was stopped for endoscopy with nothing put in its place.

What the addendum requires: such patients must either be switched to aspirin across the interruption — 300 mg loading dose the day before the anticoagulant is stopped, then 75 mg daily, continued until anticoagulation is re-established and therapeutic — or be discussed with an interventional cardiologist first.

A patient on a DOAC or warfarin alone, years after a stent, looks like a simple hold. It is not. This is the single most dangerous gap in the parent guideline. BSG addendum, June 2024

GLP-1 and SGLT-2 agents before sedation

Do not routinely hold them. The consensus across the AGA rapid update, the 2024 multisociety guidance and the 2025 ASGE position statement is fasting-first and risk-stratified, not blanket cessation:

• Continue the drug in patients without elevated aspiration risk.
• Minimum 24 hours of clear liquids before a sedated procedure — extended in symptomatic patients or those on high doses. This is the primary mitigation, not the drug hold.
• Point-of-care gastric ultrasound where gastric content status is ambiguous.
• Hold selectively — daily agents on the day of the procedure, weekly agents about 7 days — for symptomatic patients or higher-risk therapeutic procedures such as EUS, ERCP and endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty.

The ASGE 2025 position statement is the newer and more endoscopy-specific document, adds procedure-level granularity the multisociety guidance lacks, and extends the same framework to SGLT-2 inhibitors. ASGE 2025
Warfarin Coumadin · vitamin K antagonist Low-risk: Continue for low-risk
High-risk: Stop 5 days for high-risk Anticoagulant

Effective half-life 36–42 h · INR-guided

Low-risk procedure
Continue at the usual dose. Check the INR within the week before — it must be in the therapeutic range. If it is above range, delay and correct rather than proceeding.
High-risk procedure · low thrombotic risk
Stop 5 days before. Check the INR on the morning of the procedure; proceed if < 1.5.
High-risk procedure · high thrombotic risk
Stop 5 days before and bridge with LMWH. Start therapeutic LMWH 2 days after the last warfarin dose. Give the last LMWH dose ≥ 24 h before the procedure — omit the morning dose on the day. Restart warfarin the evening of the procedure and continue LMWH until the INR is back in range.
Renal function
No renal dose adjustment, but clearance falls with age, malnutrition, heart failure and liver disease — those patients often need longer than 5 days.
Restart
The evening of the procedure, at the usual maintenance dose. No loading dose. Recheck the INR within a week.
Worth knowingBridging is the most over-used manoeuvre here. In non-valvular AF the BRIDGE trial showed bridging increases major bleeding without reducing thromboembolism — restrict it to the high-risk list. Beware interactions started around the procedure: antibiotics, azoles and amiodarone all raise the INR.
Apixaban Eliquis · direct factor Xa inhibitor Low-risk: Omit the morning dose
High-risk: Last dose 3 days before Anticoagulant

Half-life 12 h · ~27% renal clearance · twice daily

Low-risk procedure
Omit the morning dose on the day of the procedure. No other change. (BSG/ESGE. The PAUSE protocol is very slightly more conservative: also omit the day before.)
High-risk procedure · low thrombotic risk
Last dose 3 days before the procedure — i.e. no drug on the two days before, and none on the day itself.
High-risk procedure · high thrombotic risk
Same omission schedule. Bridging is not required and not recommended for DOACs — their rapid onset makes it unnecessary. If the thrombotic risk is extreme, the question is whether to defer the procedure, not whether to bridge.
Renal function
CrCl 30–50: no change to the schedule above. CrCl < 30: not well studied — extend the interval and involve haematology.
Restart
24 h after a low-risk procedure · 48–72 h after a high-risk one, once haemostasis is secure. After ESD or a large EMR, consider longer.
Worth knowingPAUSE validated this simple no-testing, no-bridging strategy: 30-day major bleeding 1.35% and arterial thromboembolism 0.16%. Do not check anti-Xa levels routinely.

Aspirin low-dose 75–100 mg · irreversible COX-1 inhibitor Low-risk: Continue
High-risk: Continue — do not stop Antiplatelet

Irreversible — platelet function recovers over 7–10 days as new platelets appear

Low-risk procedure
Continue.
High-risk procedure · low thrombotic risk
Continue for essentially everything, including polypectomy, EMR, sphincterotomy and PEG. The single exception is ampullectomy, where stopping is individualised.
High-risk procedure · high thrombotic risk
Continue — absolutely. Aspirin is the drug you keep when a P2Y12 inhibitor has to be stopped.

No adjustment.

Not applicable if it was never stopped. If it was, restart within 2–3 days, sooner if the indication is secondary prevention.
Worth knowingStopping aspirin is a common reflex and usually the wrong one. In secondary prevention, interruption is associated with a rebound in cardiovascular events, and the endoscopic bleeding risk it removes is small. If a patient on aspirin monotherapy for primary prevention needs a very high-risk resection, stopping is reasonable — the indication itself is often no longer valid.

Enoxaparin half-life 4–7 h · renally cleared · anti-Xa monitoring only in special cases

Low-risk procedure
Not needed. Low-risk procedures do not require interruption of warfarin at all, so there is nothing to bridge.
High-risk procedure · low thrombotic risk
Not needed if the thrombotic risk is low — and not needed for any DOAC, at any thrombotic risk.
High-risk procedure · high thrombotic risk
Therapeutic-dose LMWH, e.g. enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily. Start 2 days after the last warfarin dose. Last dose ≥ 24 h before the procedure — omit the morning dose on the day. Restart 24–48 h afterwards once haemostasis is secure, and continue until the INR is therapeutic again.
Renal function
CrCl < 30: reduce the dose (enoxaparin 1 mg/kg once daily) or use unfractionated heparin, which is easier to stop and reverse.
Restart
24–48 h after the procedure, guided by the bleeding risk of what was actually done rather than what was planned.
Worth knowingThe evidence has moved decisively against routine bridging: more bleeding, no reduction in thromboembolism. Reserve it for the specific high-risk indications, and prefer unfractionated heparin when the situation is unstable or renal function is poor.

Acute GI bleeding on antithrombotics

Resuscitation and endoscopic haemostasis do most of the work. Reversal is an adjunct for life-threatening bleeding, not a reflex — and every reversal agent buys haemostasis at the cost of thrombosis.

Warfarin

4-factor PCC 25–50 U/kg by INR and weight, plus IV vitamin K 5–10 mg. PCC works within minutes; the vitamin K is what stops the INR rebounding as the PCC is cleared — giving one without the other is a common error.

FFP only if PCC is unavailable — slower, large volume, less complete. Do not delay endoscopy for an INR < 2.5.

Dabigatran

Idarucizumab 5 g IV, given as two 2.5 g bolus doses. Complete, specific and effective within minutes.

Haemodialysis is an effective fallback — dabigatran is minimally protein-bound. Activated charcoal if the last dose was within 2–4 h.

Apixaban · Rivaroxaban · Edoxaban

4-factor PCC 50 U/kg is the pragmatic first-line in most hospitals. Andexanet alfa is the specific agent, dosed low (400 mg bolus + 480 mg infusion) or high (800 mg + 960 mg) according to the drug, the dose and the time since the last dose.

ISTH guidance advises against andexanet for GI bleeding that local endoscopic measures can control. In ANNEXA-I it achieved better haemostasis than usual care (67% vs 53%) but with roughly double the thrombotic events (10.3% vs 5.6%), and it complicates any subsequent heparin use. Reserve it, and involve haematology.

Antiplatelet agents

There is no useful reversal agent. Platelet transfusion does not improve outcomes in antiplatelet-associated bleeding and may cause harm — do not give it reflexively.

Endoscopic haemostasis, and then getting the aspirin back on board, are what actually change outcome.

All patients

Do not give tranexamic acid for acute GI bleeding. HALT-IT randomised over 12,000 patients: no reduction in death, with more venous thromboembolism and seizures.

Resuscitate, restrict transfusion to a haemoglobin threshold of about 7–8 g/dL, treat as variceal if there is any suspicion of portal hypertension, and get to endoscopy.

Getting back on the drug

The under-appreciated risk is stopping, not restarting. Withholding aspirin after GI bleeding in a patient on secondary prevention increases mortality. Resume aspirin as soon as haemostasis is secure — usually within 3 days, often sooner.
Before stopping any antiplatelet in a patient with a drug-eluting stent under a year old, a bare-metal stent under 30 days, or an ACS within 90 days: speak to cardiology first. This is the conversation that gets skipped at 3 a.m. and causes the stent thrombosis.
Anticoagulants after a bleed. Most patients should restart — the question is when, not whether. For a bleed with an identified and treated source, restarting within about a week is usual. Involve the prescribing team, and document the reasoning either way.

About this page

Elective procedures in adults. Emergency management is on the bleeding tab.

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Adult patients, elective settings unless stated. Verify against your local protocol and the current label before acting. This page does not replace discussion with cardiology or haematology where the guidelines call for it.
Where the numbers come from. Hold and restart intervals follow the BSG/ESGE 2021 guideline update, cross-checked against ASGE guidance and the PAUSE study protocol, which validated a no-testing, no-bridging DOAC strategy in over 3,000 patients. Where BSG/ESGE and ASGE differ, the more conservative interval is shown and the difference is noted on the drug card.
A note on the ASGE antithrombotic document. It dates from 2016 (Gastrointest Endosc 2016;83:3–16) and ASGE has not replaced it — its 2024–2026 output covers other topics. It is a decade old and materially out of step with BSG/ESGE 2021 and ACG-CAG 2022 on DOAC handling. Treat it as historical context, not as a co-equal current source.
Two things this page cannot decide for you. Whether a stent is safe to leave unprotected — that is a cardiology decision. And whether an individual resection is worth the bleeding risk — that is yours, at the time, looking at the lesion.
