Making the diagnosis

IBS is a positive clinical diagnosis, made on the symptom pattern with limited testing — not a label applied once everything else has been excluded. ACG found the positive strategy reaches effective treatment faster and costs less, at high certainty.

New — released May 2026

Rome V

Recurrent abdominal pain or discomfort — discomfort is back after being removed in Rome IV — on at least 3 days per month, associated with two or more of: related to defaecation · a change in stool frequency · a change in stool form. Symptoms must be intermittent rather than continuous, which is what separates IBS from centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome.

Rome V also splits clinical criteria from research criteria for the first time. The clinical version asks whether symptoms are bothersome — interfering with daily activities or prompting the patient to seek help — and suggests a minimum duration of about 8 weeks rather than 6 months.

Rome IV — 2016, still in use in most services

The stricter version

Recurrent abdominal pain (discomfort excluded) on at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, with two or more of the same three features, and symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.

Why this matters practically. Lowering the threshold from weekly pain to three days a month, and readmitting discomfort, roughly doubles measured IBS prevalence — from around 4% under Rome IV to about 9% under Rome V. Patients who did not formally qualify before now do. Know which criteria your service, and any trial you are reading, is using.

Subtype — it determines the treatment

Based on the Bristol stool scale, counting only days with abnormal bowel movements. Ask the patient to record a stool diary for two weeks; recall is unreliable.

IBS-C

More than 25% of abnormal stools are Bristol 1–2, and fewer than 25% are Bristol 6–7.

IBS-D

More than 25% are Bristol 6–7, and fewer than 25% are Bristol 1–2.

IBS-M

More than 25% in both directions.

IBS-U

Meets IBS criteria but the stool pattern fits none of the above.

Alarm features — none of this applies if any are present

Investigate rather than diagnose IBS
  • Age of onset 50 or overThe single most useful discriminator
  • Rectal bleeding or melaenaUnless clearly from haemorrhoids or an anal fissure
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Nocturnal symptoms — waking from sleep to pass stool or with painFunctional symptoms respect sleep; organic disease does not
  • Family history of colorectal cancer, IBD or coeliac disease
  • Iron deficiency anaemia, or any abnormal blood test
  • Abdominal or rectal mass, or any abnormal examination finding
  • Progressive, unremitting symptoms rather than a relapsing–remitting course
  • Recent onset in an older patient with no prior history
Say the diagnosis out loud. A confident, explained, positive diagnosis is itself an intervention — it reduces further testing, improves symptoms and improves satisfaction. Explain it as a real disorder of gut–brain signalling with altered motility and visceral hypersensitivity, not as "nothing found". "Your tests are all normal" is heard as "I don't believe you".
The whole point of limited testing is that a positive diagnosis plus a small panel is as safe as an exhaustive workup, and considerably faster and cheaper. Each additional normal test buys diminishing reassurance and raises the patient's suspicion that something is being missed.
Coeliac serology — tTG-IgA with total IgA
Strong · moderate quality
In everyone with IBS and diarrhoea. Coeliac disease is several times more common in this population, and it is the diagnosis you cannot afford to miss. Total IgA must be sent alongside — selective IgA deficiency renders the tTG falsely negative, and it is over-represented in coeliac disease.
Faecal calprotectin and CRP
Strong · moderate to very low quality
In patients with diarrhoea and no alarm features, to exclude inflammatory bowel disease. A normal calprotectin in this setting has a high negative predictive value and is what allows you to avoid colonoscopy in a young patient.
Complete blood count
Standard practice
Anaemia converts an IBS presentation into something else.
Anorectal physiology testing
Consensus recommendation
For symptoms suggesting a pelvic floor disorder, or constipation refractory to standard treatment. Anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion identifies dyssynergic defaecation — which does not respond to laxatives or secretagogues, but does respond to biofeedback. This is the commonest reversible cause of "refractory IBS-C".
Do not Routine colonoscopy under age 45 without alarm features
Conditional against · low quality
The yield is very low, and a normal colonoscopy does not reassure the patient for long. Reserve it for alarm features, or a raised risk of microscopic colitis — female, age 50 or over, autoimmune disease, weight loss, nocturnal or severe watery diarrhoea, or diarrhoea for less than 12 months.
Do not Routine stool testing for enteric pathogens
Conditional against · low quality
Not in everyone. Test when there is a relevant exposure or travel history, or when the illness began acutely after an enteric infection.
Do not Food allergy and food sensitivity testing
Consensus against
IgG food panels, hair analysis and similar have no diagnostic validity and generate needless dietary restriction. The exception is a genuinely reproducible reaction to a specific food, which deserves proper allergy assessment.
Do not Routine testing for SIBO, pancreatic insufficiency or carbohydrate intolerance
BSG — against in typical IBS
Breath testing for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth remains contentious and is not part of the standard workup. Faecal elastase and lactose or fructose breath tests belong to specific clinical suspicions, not to the routine panel.
Do not Routine thyroid function, imaging or repeat testing in stable disease
Low yield
Once a positive diagnosis is made and initial tests are normal, repeating them because symptoms persist rewards neither of you. Re-investigate for a change in pattern or a new alarm feature — not for continuing symptoms.
Three societies, three slightly different answers. ACG 2021, AGA 2022 and BSG 2021 largely agree, but differ on antispasmodics, PEG and SSRIs. Where they diverge, both positions are shown — the disagreement is usually about which question was asked, not about the underlying data.
Soluble fibre — psyllium or ispaghula Strong for
ACG strong · moderate quality
Start low, around 3–4 g/day, and build up over weeks — going in fast causes bloating and the patient abandons it. Insoluble fibre such as wheat bran makes IBS worse and should be actively stopped. This distinction is the whole recommendation.
Low FODMAP diet Conditional for
ACG conditional · very low quality
A limited trial, in three phases: strict restriction for 4–6 weeks, then systematic reintroduction to find the individual triggers, then long-term personalisation. It is not a permanent diet. Dietitian-led delivery works considerably better than a handout, and indefinite restriction risks nutritional deficiency and disordered eating.
Peppermint oil Conditional for
ACG conditional · low quality
Enteric-coated, before meals. Cheap, well tolerated, and worth trying early. Heartburn is the usual limitation — the enteric coating exists to reduce it.
Tricyclic antidepressants Strong for
ACG strong · moderate quality
The single most useful drug class across IBS. Amitriptyline 10 mg at night, titrated slowly to 30–50 mg as tolerated. Prescribed as a gut–brain neuromodulator, not an antidepressant — say so explicitly, or the patient will read the leaflet and stop. The anticholinergic effect makes them particularly suited to IBS-D and unhelpful in IBS-C.
Gut-directed psychotherapy — CBT and hypnotherapy Conditional for
ACG conditional · very low quality
Gut-directed hypnotherapy and IBS-specific CBT have durable effects, including in refractory disease. Offer them as a treatment for a gut disorder, not as an implication that the symptoms are imagined — how this is framed determines whether the patient accepts it.
Exercise and sleep Conditional for
BSG
Regular physical activity improves global symptoms and is one of the few interventions with no downside.
Antispasmodics — hyoscine, dicyclomine Societies differ
ACG conditional AGAINST · AGA conditional FOR · BSG first-line
A genuine three-way disagreement. ACG advises against the agents available in the US on the strength of the trial data; AGA and BSG both support them for pain and cramping. In practice they are cheap, familiar and useful as needed for cramping — a reasonable early trial while acknowledging thin evidence.
Probiotics Recommended against
ACG conditional AGAINST · very low quality
The trials are numerous, small, heterogeneous in strain and dose, and mostly commercially sponsored. There is no basis for recommending a particular product. Patients will ask; the honest answer is that the evidence does not support a specific recommendation.
Faecal microbiota transplantation Recommended against
ACG strong AGAINST · very low quality
Not a treatment for IBS outside a trial.
Linaclotide Strong for
ACG strong · high quality · AGA strong
A guanylate cyclase-C agonist. The best-evidenced drug in IBS-C, and the only IBS drug with a strong recommendation from both societies at high certainty. Improves pain as well as bowel habit — which laxatives do not. Diarrhoea is the dose-limiting effect; take on an empty stomach 30 minutes before the first meal.
Plecanatide Conditional for
ACG strong (class) · AGA conditional
Same mechanism as linaclotide, possibly with less diarrhoea. Reasonable if linaclotide is not tolerated.
Lubiprostone Strong for
ACG strong · moderate quality
A chloride channel activator. 8 µg twice daily with food for IBS-C — note this is a lower dose than for chronic idiopathic constipation. Nausea is the main limitation and is reduced by taking it with food.
Tenapanor Conditional for
AGA conditional
An NHE3 inhibitor — a different mechanism again, useful when the secretagogues fail or are not tolerated.
Tegaserod Restricted use
ACG · restricted population
A 5-HT4 agonist, reintroduced with restrictions after withdrawal for cardiovascular safety. Women under 65 with no more than one cardiovascular risk factor and no history of ischaemic events, who have failed secretagogues.
Polyethylene glycol Societies differ
ACG conditional AGAINST · AGA conditional FOR
The societies differ because they are answering different questions. PEG reliably improves stool frequency and consistency but does not improve abdominal pain, and ACG judges global symptoms. Useful, cheap, and worth using for the constipation — just do not expect it to treat the pain, and do not stop there if pain is the dominant complaint.
Rifaximin Strong for
ACG strong · moderate quality · AGA conditional
550 mg three times daily for 14 days. A two-week course with benefit lasting months in responders. Up to two retreatment courses are supported for those who responded initially and then relapsed. Minimally absorbed, so systemic effects are few.
Eluxadoline Conditional for
ACG conditional · moderate quality · AGA conditional
100 mg twice daily, or 75 mg twice daily if not tolerated or with mild–moderate hepatic impairment. Absolutely contraindicated after cholecystectomy and in those drinking more than three alcoholic drinks daily — pancreatitis and sphincter of Oddi spasm. Asking about the gallbladder before prescribing is not optional.
Loperamide Conditional for
AGA conditional · very low quality
Improves stool frequency and consistency, not pain. Best used pre-emptively before a meal or an outing rather than reactively. BSG places it first-line for diarrhoea; ACG did not find evidence for global symptom benefit.
Alosetron Restricted use
ACG conditional · restricted
A 5-HT3 antagonist. 0.5 mg twice daily, increased to 1 mg twice daily after 4 weeks if needed. Restricted to women with severe IBS-D refractory to conventional therapy because of ischaemic colitis and severe constipation. Effective when appropriately targeted.
Bile acid sequestrants Societies differ
ACG conditional AGAINST · widely used
ACG advises against them for global IBS-D symptoms on insufficient evidence. But bile acid diarrhoea accounts for a substantial share of what is labelled IBS-D, and in that group sequestrants work well. The better framing: this is not an IBS treatment, it is treatment of a mimic — see the Refractory tab.
SSRIs Recommended against
AGA conditional AGAINST · low quality
AGA advises against them for both IBS-C and IBS-D. BSG still permits them second-line in primary care. Where an SSRI is genuinely indicated for coexisting anxiety or depression, treat that on its own merits — just do not expect it to treat the bowel symptoms.

When it isn't working

Before escalating treatment, revisit the diagnosis. A meaningful proportion of "refractory IBS" is a different condition that responds well to its own treatment.

Bile acid diarrhoea

Up to a quarter to a third of people labelled IBS-D

Watery, urgent, often postprandial diarrhoea; worse after cholecystectomy, ileal resection or Crohn's. Test with SeHCAT retention where available, or serum 7αC4 or FGF19. A therapeutic trial of colestyramine is a reasonable pragmatic alternative. The most commonly missed diagnosis in this whole area.

Microscopic colitis

Chronic watery diarrhoea with a normal-looking colon

Suspect in women aged 50 or over, autoimmune disease, nocturnal or severe watery diarrhoea, recent onset, or use of PPIs, NSAIDs or SSRIs. Diagnosis requires colonoscopy with random biopsies from right and left colon — the mucosa looks normal, so the endoscopist must be told what is being looked for.

Coeliac disease

Several times more prevalent than in the general population

Serology must be taken on a gluten-containing diet. If the patient has already excluded gluten, either reintroduce before testing or check HLA-DQ2/DQ8 to exclude it.

Inflammatory bowel disease

Calprotectin is the screening test

A raised calprotectin in a young patient with diarrhoea warrants colonoscopy, not a repeat calprotectin.

Dyssynergic defaecation

The commonest reversible cause of refractory IBS-C

Straining, incomplete evacuation, digitation, a sensation of blockage. Does not respond to laxatives or secretagogues; does respond to biofeedback, with good durable results. Diagnose with anorectal manometry and balloon expulsion.

Carbohydrate malabsorption

Lactose, fructose, sorbitol

Often revealed by the reintroduction phase of a low FODMAP diet, which is a more useful test than breath testing.

Medication effects

Frequently overlooked

Metformin, magnesium, PPIs, SSRIs, NSAIDs, opioids — opioid-induced constipation and narcotic bowel syndrome are their own entities and will not respond to IBS treatment while the opioid continues.

Gynaecological disease

Endometriosis in particular

Cyclical symptoms, dyspareunia, or pain that tracks the menstrual cycle. Endometriosis is commonly misdiagnosed as IBS for years.

Giardiasis and other chronic infection

Where the exposure fits

Consider with relevant travel, well water or contact history, particularly if the onset was abrupt.

Centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome

Continuous, not related to defaecation

Rome V explicitly distinguishes this from IBS: pain that is continuous rather than intermittent and not clearly tied to bowel function is a different disorder needing a different approach — neuromodulators and psychological therapy rather than gut-targeted drugs.

If the diagnosis still holds

Escalate deliberately

Optimise the first-line measures properly before adding drugs — soluble fibre titrated slowly, a dietitian-led FODMAP trial with a real reintroduction phase, and a neuromodulator at an adequate dose for an adequate time. Most "failed" treatment is under-dosed or abandoned early.

Add a brain–gut therapy

Gut-directed hypnotherapy and IBS-specific CBT have the best evidence in refractory disease, including delivered remotely. This is where the largest untapped benefit usually sits.

Reconsider severity and comorbidity

Severe IBS travels with anxiety, depression, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue and a history of adverse life events. Treating the bowel alone will not move a patient whose central sensitisation is the dominant process. Multidisciplinary care beats sequential monotherapy.

Do not keep re-investigating. Once the diagnosis is secure and initial tests were normal, repeating them because symptoms persist is a well-described trap: it reinforces the patient's belief that something has been missed and delays effective treatment. Re-investigate for a change in the pattern or a new alarm feature — not for persistence.

Follow-up & the long view

Set the expectation early

IBS is chronic and relapsing–remitting. The goal is control and function, not cure. Patients told this at the outset cope far better than those who expect eradication and interpret every flare as failure.

Review at 4–8 weeks after any change

Long enough to judge fibre, a FODMAP trial or a neuromodulator; short enough that a patient who is getting nowhere is not left for months.

The relationship is part of the treatment

Continuity, taking the symptoms seriously, and an explanation the patient can repeat to their family do measurable work. This is one of the few areas where the consultation itself is as effective as the prescription.

About this page

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Adults. Drug availability differs substantially between countries — tegaserod, alosetron, eluxadoline and tenapanor are not available everywhere, and some antispasmodics available in Europe are not licensed in the US. Check your local formulary.
