Acute hepatitis B
HBsAg + · anti-HBc IgM + · anti-HBs − · HBV DNA usually high
Most immunocompetent adults clear it. Antivirals are not routinely indicated — treat only severe or fulminant disease (coagulopathy, jaundice beyond 4 weeks, or acute liver failure) or a protracted course. Repeat HBsAg at 6 months: still positive means chronic infection.
Chronic hepatitis B infection
HBsAg + for > 6 months · anti-HBc IgG + · anti-HBs −
The starting point for everything on the Treat tab. Next steps: HBeAg and anti-HBe, quantitative HBV DNA, ALT, fibrosis assessment, HDV and HIV serology, HAV immunity, and liver ultrasound.
Resolved past infection
HBsAg − · anti-HBs + · anti-HBc +
Immune through natural infection. Not infectious in ordinary contact, but the virus persists as cccDNA in hepatocytes — this patient can still reactivate under immunosuppression. That is why anti-HBc is part of pre-biologic screening, not just HBsAg.
Vaccinated
HBsAg − · anti-HBs + (≥ 10 mIU/mL) · anti-HBc −
Immunity from vaccine, never infected. No reactivation risk. The absent anti-HBc is what distinguishes this from resolved infection.
Isolated anti-HBc
HBsAg − · anti-HBs − · anti-HBc +
Three possibilities: resolved infection with waned anti-HBs (commonest), a false positive, or occult HBV infection. Check HBV DNA. Treat as at risk of reactivation before immunosuppression — this is the pattern people miss. Consider vaccinating and checking the anti-HBs response: a brisk anamnestic rise favours past infection.
Occult HBV infection
HBsAg − · anti-HBc + · HBV DNA detectable (usually < 200 IU/mL)
Relevant to reactivation risk, to blood and organ donation, and to HCC risk in advanced fibrosis. Not usually treated in its own right.
Susceptible — never infected, not immune
HBsAg − · anti-HBs − · anti-HBc −
Vaccinate. Particularly before starting immunosuppression, and in every patient with chronic liver disease.
HBeAg-positive chronic infection — EASL phase 1
HBeAg + · HBV DNA > 10⁷ IU/mL · ALT normal · minimal fibrosis · HBsAg high
The old "immune tolerant" phase. Highly infectious but little liver injury. AASLD 2025 recommends against treating under age 40, and shared decision-making above 40 or with ≥ F2 fibrosis or grade ≥ 2 inflammation — a change from previous observation-only advice.
HBeAg-positive chronic hepatitis — EASL phase 2
HBeAg + · HBV DNA 10⁴–10⁷ IU/mL · ALT elevated · moderate–severe activity
The immune-active phase. Treat. This is where fibrosis accumulates.
HBeAg-negative chronic infection — EASL phase 3
HBeAg − · anti-HBe + · HBV DNA < 2,000 IU/mL · ALT normal · qHBsAg < 1,000 IU/mL
The old "inactive carrier". Best prognosis of any chronic phase, but not a discharge: 1–3% per year lose HBsAg, and a minority revert to active hepatitis. Keep monitoring, and still consider HCC surveillance if other risk factors are present.
HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis — EASL phase 4
HBeAg − · anti-HBe + · HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL · ALT elevated, often fluctuating
Treat. Fluctuating ALT and DNA mean a single normal set of results does not exclude this — serial testing over 6–12 months is what makes the diagnosis.
Indeterminate — the "grey zone"
Does not fit cleanly into any phase — e.g. DNA 2,000–20,000 with normal ALT, or DNA < 2,000 with raised ALT
Roughly 40% of patients sit here. EASL 2025 discourages the "grey zone" label and asks for a personalised assessment instead; AASLD 2025 suggests treatment through shared decision-making, favouring it with age > 40, male sex, or platelets < 180,000. Do not simply keep observing indefinitely — that is the trap this category creates.
The ALT upper limit that matters. AASLD uses 35 U/L for men and 25 U/L for women — well below most laboratory reference ranges. Using the lab's printed ULN systematically under-diagnoses active disease, particularly in women.

Who to treat

Both AASLD/IDSA and EASL published major updates in 2025, and both moved in the same direction: treat more people, earlier. If your practice is anchored to the 2017–2018 criteria, this is the part that has changed.

EASL 2025, in one line: in principle every HBsAg-positive person with detectable HBV DNA is a candidate for therapy — the decision is then shaped by DNA level, ALT, fibrosis stage and HCC risk, not by phase labels.
AASLD 2025 mnemonic — FAST: Fibrosis, Age and Sex determine Treatment. It exists to catch the patients the old phase-based criteria excluded, particularly the 40% who never fitted a phase cleanly.

Immune-tolerant phase — HBeAg-positive, very high DNA, normal ALT

AASLD 2025

Under 40: recommended against treating. Over 40, or ≥ F2 fibrosis or grade ≥ 2 inflammation: shared decision-making, leaning toward treatment. The rationale for treating earlier is reduced HBV DNA integration and clonal hepatocyte expansion; the counterweight is decades of daily medication.

Indeterminate phase

AASLD 2025, conditional

Suggest treatment through shared decision-making in HBeAg-negative patients without cirrhosis. Factors that push toward treating: age over 40, male sex, platelets under 180,000, family history of HCC, persistent low-level ALT elevation.

Persistently low DNA (< 2,000) with persistently raised ALT

EASL 2025, weak

Consider treatment — but first exclude the other causes of a raised ALT, which in 2026 usually means metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease or alcohol.

Do not treat — monitor

Acute hepatitis B in an immunocompetent adult

Uncomplicated

Over 95% clear it spontaneously. Treat only severe, fulminant or protracted disease. Recheck HBsAg at 6 months.

HBeAg-negative chronic infection — inactive carrier

DNA < 2,000, normal ALT, no fibrosis

Monitor rather than treat. Reassess at least annually, and reconsider if the DNA climbs, the ALT rises, or fibrosis appears.

Before starting, complete the picture: HDV serology in every HBsAg-positive patient at least once · HIV and HCV · HAV immunity and vaccination · fibrosis staging by elastography or non-invasive score · baseline ultrasound · family history of HCC · alcohol and metabolic risk · and screening plus vaccination of household and sexual contacts.

Stopping treatment

The 2025 position: mostly, don't

AASLD 2025 conditionally recommends against stopping nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy until HBsAg loss — a deliberate move away from the previous willingness to stop after seroconversion or a period of suppression. Most patients are on treatment indefinitely.

If stopping is nevertheless considered

Restrict it to non-cirrhotic patients meeting strict criteria: quantitative HBsAg < 100 IU/mL, at least 2 years of undetectable HBV DNA, and for HBeAg-positive patients, seroconversion followed by at least 12 months of consolidation. Never stop in cirrhosis.

If therapy stops, watch closely

ALT and HBV DNA monthly for the first 3–6 months, then every 3 months. Relapse is common and flares can be severe. Have a restart threshold agreed in advance rather than deciding in the middle of one.

Functional cure

Sustained HBsAg loss with HBV DNA below the limit of quantification, at least 24 weeks off therapy. Anti-HBs is not required. It happens in well under 1% per year on nucleos(t)ide analogues — which is precisely why the finite-therapy pipeline exists.

HCC surveillance

Ultrasound every 6 months, with or without AFP. The interval is 6 months in every group below — the question is only who is included.
Surveillance indicated
  • Any cirrhosis, compensated or not — including after HBV DNA becomes undetectableSuppression reduces HCC risk but does not abolish it

  • HBV–HDV coinfection, regardless of cirrhosis
  • HBV–HIV coinfection from about age 30
  • Family history of HCC in a first-degree relative
  • Asian men over 40 and Asian women over 50 · people of African origin from around age 20
  • Continue after HBsAg loss in men over 40 and women over 50, and in anyone with established cirrhosis
Risk scores such as PAGE-B and REACH-B can refine the decision in non-cirrhotic patients, but no guideline mandates one. Age, cirrhosis, DNA level, family history, genotype and metabolic comorbidity are the drivers.

Reactivation, pregnancy & coinfection

Reactivation before immunosuppression

Screen everyone before starting any immunosuppressive or biologic therapy — HBsAg, anti-HBc and anti-HBs. Screening on HBsAg alone misses the anti-HBc-positive patient, who is the one who reactivates on rituximab. This applies directly to the biologics on the IBD page.
HBsAg-positive · high-risk drug
Antiviral prophylaxis — strong recommendation
  • B-cell depleting agents — rituximab, ofatumumab, obinutuzumab
  • CAR-T cell therapy
  • Anthracyclines
  • Anti-TNF agents
  • Anti-IL-6 — tocilizumab
  • Ustekinumab, secukinumab

  • CAR-T cell therapy
  • Haematopoietic stem cell transplant

Monitoring alone — conditional

Which drug, and for how long. Use entecavir, TDF or TAF — not lamivudine, even for short courses. Continue for at least 6 months after the immunosuppression ends, extended to 12 months after B-cell depleting agents. Patients who meet independent treatment criteria simply stay on therapy.

Pregnancy

Preventing mother-to-child transmission

Check HBV DNA in the second trimester. If it exceeds 200,000 IU/mL, start TDF at week 28 and continue to delivery or a few weeks after. TDF has the most safety data in pregnancy; TAF is an alternative where TDF is unsuitable.

The newborn

HBIG plus the first hepatitis B vaccine dose within 12 hours of birth, regardless of maternal viral load, then complete the vaccine series. This alone prevents most transmission; antivirals close the residual gap at high viral loads. Check the infant's HBsAg and anti-HBs at 9–12 months.

Other practical points

Breastfeeding is not contraindicated, including on TDF. Caesarean section is not indicated to prevent transmission. Postpartum ALT flares occur in up to a quarter of women — check ALT and DNA at about 6 and 12 weeks after delivery, whether or not antivirals were used.

Coinfection

Hepatitis D

Test every HBsAg-positive patient for anti-HDV at least once. It is the most aggressive form of chronic viral hepatitis, and it is routinely missed because nobody sends the test. Confirm a positive antibody with HDV RNA. Refer to a specialist centre.

This changed after every guideline on this page was written. Bulevirtide (Hepcludex) received FDA accelerated approval on 22 May 2026 — 8.5 mg subcutaneously once daily, for adults with chronic hepatitis D without cirrhosis or with compensated cirrhosis. It is the first and only HDV therapy approved in the United States; it has been available in the EU since 2020. Approval rested on MYR301, showing a significant combined virological and biochemical response at week 48, sustained through 144 weeks.

The AASLD 2025 guideline was finalised roughly six months before this approval and its HDV recommendations were written when no HDV drug was licensed in the US. Any HDV advice taken from that document alone is now out of date.

HIV

Every HBV patient needs an HIV test before starting antivirals. Coinfected patients need a full antiretroviral regimen containing tenofovir plus emtricitabine or lamivudine — never HBV monotherapy, which selects HIV resistance.

Hepatitis C

HBV can reactivate during direct-acting antiviral therapy for HCV. Check HBsAg and anti-HBc before starting DAAs; HBsAg-positive patients need prophylaxis or treatment alongside.

Prevention

Vaccinate all susceptible household and sexual contacts, and screen them. Vaccinate every patient with chronic liver disease against hepatitis A and B. In immunocompromised patients and those on dialysis, check anti-HBs after the series and use higher-dose or adjuvanted schedules.

About this page

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Adults, non-transplant setting. Verify doses against the current local label. Decompensated cirrhosis, pregnancy and transplant candidacy all warrant hepatology involvement rather than a protocol.
Both major guidelines changed in 2025 and broadened treatment eligibility in the same direction. Where AASLD/IDSA and EASL differ in emphasis, this page says so; where they agree, only one number is given.
