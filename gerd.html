Diagnosing GERD

Classic heartburn or regurgitation without alarm features needs no test to start: an 8-week trial of a once-daily PPI taken 30–60 minutes before a meal is both the treatment and the diagnostic step. Everything else on this page is for when that does not settle it.

Alarm features — scope first, do not treat empirically

Endoscopy is first-line
  • DysphagiaEndoscopy first — do not treat empirically
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Gastrointestinal bleeding or iron deficiency anaemia
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Multiple risk factors for Barrett's oesophagusSee the Barrett's tab for the criteria
  • Age over 60 with new-onset symptoms
  • Family history of oesophageal or gastric cancer
Perform endoscopy off PPI. Stop acid suppression for 2–4 weeks beforehand — on treatment, erosive oesophagitis heals and eosinophilic oesophagitis can be masked, and a normal-looking scope tells you nothing.

Los Angeles classification — and what each grade means

Grade A
One or more mucosal breaks ≤ 5 mm, not extending between the tops of two folds
Not conclusive for GERD. Grade A is seen in healthy controls and does not establish the diagnosis on its own — this is the point most often missed when a report says "grade A oesophagitis".
Grade B
One or more mucosal breaks > 5 mm, not continuous between the tops of two folds
Now conclusive. Lyon Consensus 2.0 lowered the bar from grade C to grade B, on the basis that grade B is highly uncommon in healthy controls. This meaningfully expands who has a confirmed diagnosis without further testing.
Grade C
Mucosal breaks continuous between the tops of two or more folds, involving < 75% of the circumference
Conclusive. Also mandates indefinite maintenance PPI — a strong recommendation — and means reflux testing off therapy is unnecessary and inappropriate.
Grade D
Mucosal breaks involving ≥ 75% of the oesophageal circumference
Conclusive. Same implications as grade C. Repeat endoscopy after healing to exclude underlying Barrett's.

Lyon Consensus 2.0 — reflux testing thresholds

Test off therapy when the diagnosis is unestablished and endoscopy was normal. Test on therapy when GERD is already documented and symptoms persist. Do not test off therapy at all in LA grade C or D or long-segment Barrett's — the diagnosis is already conclusive.

Acid exposure time > 6%
Conclusive GERD
On wireless pH monitoring, present on at least 2 days. This is actionable disease — escalate treatment or consider a procedure.
Acid exposure time < 4%
Excludes GERD
On all days, with negative symptom association. Look for another explanation — functional heartburn, eosinophilic oesophagitis, a motility disorder, or a rumination syndrome.
Acid exposure time 4–6%
Inconclusive
The grey zone. Resolve it with adjunctive metrics — mean nocturnal baseline impedance, symptom association, hiatal hernia, a hypotensive oesophagogastric junction, or ineffective motility.
Mean nocturnal baseline impedance < 1500 Ω
Supports pathological reflux
A measure of chronic mucosal integrity, less affected by day-to-day variability than acid exposure time.
Mean nocturnal baseline impedance > 2500 Ω
Argues against GERD
A new exclusion criterion in Lyon 2.0. Regional variation exists — validation studies in Asian populations suggest higher thresholds.
On therapy: AET > 4% and > 80 reflux episodes/day
True refractory reflux
Measured on antisecretory therapy in a patient with already-documented GERD. This identifies the small group who genuinely need escalation or an antireflux procedure.
What Lyon 2.0 changed. LA grade B now counts as conclusive endoscopic evidence — previously it took grade C. Mean nocturnal baseline impedance above 2500 Ω was added as an exclusion criterion. And 96-hour wireless monitoring is preferred over 48 hours, because a third of patients show no consistent pattern across only two days.
High-resolution manometry is not a test for GERD. It neither makes nor excludes the diagnosis. Its role is to exclude achalasia and absent contractility and to assess contractile reserve before an antireflux procedure.

The four phenotypes — they need different treatment

Erosive reflux disease

LA grade B, C or D on endoscopy

Diagnosis complete. No reflux testing needed. Grades C and D require indefinite maintenance therapy.

Non-erosive reflux disease

Normal endoscopy, abnormal acid exposure time

Real GERD without visible damage. Responds to acid suppression; suitable for on-demand or intermittent therapy once controlled.

Reflux hypersensitivity

Normal acid exposure time, positive symptom association

Physiological reflux perceived as symptomatic. Acid suppression alone often disappoints — neuromodulators and brain–gut therapies do the work here.

Functional heartburn

Normal acid exposure time, negative symptom association

Not a reflux disease at all. Stop the PPI — a key concept in the guideline when off-therapy testing is negative and there is no prior GERD diagnosis. Treat as a disorder of gut–brain interaction.

The most valuable line in the whole guideline: take the PPI 30–60 minutes before a meal, not at bedtime and not with food. The pump has to be active for the drug to bind it. A large share of "PPI failure" is a timing failure.

PPI safety, in proportion. Scrutiny of long-term PPI use has grown, but the guideline is clear that they remain first-line and that the observational associations have not been shown to be causal. Use the lowest effective dose, individualised — and do not withhold a PPI from someone who needs it. Evidence for clinically meaningful rebound after abrupt discontinuation is weak.
Extra-oesophageal symptoms — cough, hoarseness, throat clearing, asthma. Evaluate non-reflux causes first (strong recommendation). Do not diagnose laryngopharyngeal reflux on laryngoscopy alone — the findings correlate poorly with symptoms. Salivary pepsin and oropharyngeal pH monitoring are not recommended.

Refractory symptoms

Only 21% of patients with PPI-refractory heartburn turn out to have genuinely refractory reflux disease. The remaining four in five have something else — which is why the sequence below starts with optimisation and diagnosis, not with escalation.

Step 1 — optimise before investigating
  • Confirm adherence — a large share of "refractory" patients are not taking it
  • Confirm timing: 30–60 minutes before a meal, not at bedtime
  • Consider twice-daily dosing, or a single switch to another PPI
  • Reconsider whether the symptom was ever reflux — only 21% of PPI-refractory heartburn turns out to be genuinely refractory reflux disease
Step 2 — endoscopy off PPI
  • Stop the PPI for 2–4 weeks first, to maximise yield
  • Looking for erosive oesophagitis, Barrett's, and — importantly — eosinophilic oesophagitis, which is a common and frequently missed explanation
  • Take biopsies even if the mucosa looks normal
Step 3 — reflux testing, matched to the question
  • Diagnosis not yet established → pH or pH-impedance monitoring off therapy, after 7–14 days off PPI
  • GERD already documented with persistent symptoms → impedance-pH on therapy, to look for ongoing acid or non-acid reflux
  • Do not test off therapy in LA grade C or D oesophagitis or long-segment Barrett's — the diagnosis is already conclusive
  • Wireless pH over 96 hours gives the highest diagnostic accuracy; 35% of patients show no consistent pattern over just 2 days
Step 4 — high-resolution manometry
  • Not a diagnostic test for GERD — it does not make or exclude the diagnosis
  • Its role is to exclude achalasia and absent contractility, and to assess contractile reserve, before any antireflux procedure
  • A motility disorder is found in 1–3% of refractory cases
Step 5 — act on the phenotype
  • Objective reflux persisting on therapy → escalate, or consider a procedure
  • Normal acid exposure with positive symptom association → reflux hypersensitivity: neuromodulators, brain–gut therapy
  • Normal acid exposure with negative symptom association → functional heartburn: stop the PPI and treat as a gut–brain disorder

Procedures

Laparoscopic fundoplication

Strong · moderate quality

For objective evidence of GERD — particularly LA grade C or D, large hiatal hernia, or persistent troublesome symptoms. Modern population data: 17.7% recurrence, 4.1% acute complications, 0.1% mortality. Over 80% achieve long-term freedom from PPIs, and satisfaction exceeds chronic medical therapy.

Magnetic sphincter augmentation

Strong · moderate quality

A reasonable alternative to fundoplication for regurgitation-predominant PPI-refractory GERD. Two randomised trials show superiority over continued medical therapy. Device erosion 0.3% at 4 years. Contraindicated with MRI above 1.5 Tesla — worth telling the patient.

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass

Conditional · low quality

For patients with obesity who are willing to accept the risks and the lifestyle requirements. Treats both problems.

Transoral incisionless fundoplication

Conditional · low quality

For regurgitation or heartburn without severe oesophagitis (LA C or D) and without a hiatal hernia larger than 2 cm.

Radiofrequency — Stretta

Cannot be recommended

Efficacy data are inconsistent and variable.

Before any procedure

Key concept

High-resolution manometry is mandatory to exclude achalasia and absent contractility and to assess contractile reserve with provocative testing. And the diagnosis must be secure: extensive evaluation is required to confirm that reflux actually causes the symptoms before committing to an irreversible operation.

Key concept: stop the PPI in patients whose off-therapy testing is negative, unless a prior GERD diagnosis has been established. Continuing acid suppression in functional heartburn is common, ineffective, and delays the treatment that would help.

Barrett's oesophagus

Definition

At least 1 cm of salmon-coloured columnar mucosa in the tubular oesophagus, with intestinal metaplasia — goblet cells — on biopsy. Both halves are required. Under 1 cm is not Barrett's, and a normal-looking squamocolumnar junction should not be biopsied.

Who to screen

Chronic GERD symptoms plus 3 or more of these
  • Male sex
  • Age over 50
  • White race
  • Tobacco smokingCurrent or past
  • Obesity — particularly central adiposity
  • Family history of Barrett's or oesophageal adenocarcinoma in a first-degree relative
A single screening endoscopy — and do not repeat it after a negative examination. The yield on repeat screening is only 2–4%.

A swallowable non-endoscopic capsule sponge device with a biomarker — Cytosponge-TFF3 or methylated DNA markers — is an acceptable alternative to endoscopy in this population, and is the practical route where endoscopic capacity is the limiting factor.

Surveillance & dysplasia

Any grade of dysplasia must be confirmed by a second pathologist with gastrointestinal expertise — a strong recommendation. Community-diagnosed dysplasia is frequently downstaged on expert review, and the grade decides between a five-year interval and an ablation programme.
Columnar-lined oesophagus < 1 cm, or an irregular Z-line
No surveillance at all
New in the AGA 2025 guideline — a conditional recommendation against endoscopic surveillance, and the most practice-changing item in it. ESGE agrees and adds that no routine biopsies are needed either. This is the single commonest source of unnecessary surveillance programmes.
Non-dysplastic Barrett's, segment < 3 cm
Every 5 years
The interval most often shortened in practice without justification. AGA 2025 phrases it as a default of 3 years that may be extended to 5 years in lower-risk patients, for instance short segments under 3 cm; ACG and ESGE state 5 years as the standard for short segments. Annual progression is 0.06% for short segments against 0.31% for long.
Non-dysplastic Barrett's, segment ≥ 3 cm
Every 3 years
Segment length is an independent predictor of progression, which is why it drives the interval. All three societies agree on 3 years here, and on 3 cm as the short-versus-long cutoff. AGA attaches a condition worth reading: the 3-year interval assumes a high-quality endoscopic examination was performed.
Indefinite for dysplasia — confirmed by an expert
Optimise PPI, repeat at 3 to 6 months
Intensify antireflux therapy to twice-daily PPI first — inflammation is a common cause of the reading. AGA 2025 says repeat at 3 months on twice-daily PPI; ACG says within 6 months — a real difference, with AGA the tighter standard. Then annual surveillance until the grade changes. Progression risk is around 1.5% per year, similar to low-grade dysplasia. Eradication therapy is not recommended for confirmed indefinite-for-dysplasia.
Low-grade dysplasia — confirmed by an expert
Endoscopic eradication preferred
Expert-confirmed LGD progresses at about 9.1% per year — far higher than non-dysplastic disease, and the reason eradication is now preferred. If surveillance is chosen instead: 6 months, 12 months, then annually.

Endoscopic eradication therapy

Resect first, then ablate

Endoscopic resection of any visible lesion must precede ablation of the flat segment. Resection gives staging information — depth of invasion, lymphovascular invasion, differentiation — that ablation destroys. Treat at a high-volume centre.

Ablation

Radiofrequency ablation is the standard. Cryotherapy is a reasonable substitute in cases resistant to radiofrequency. The endpoint is complete eradication of dysplasia and of intestinal metaplasia.

Surveillance after complete eradication

This is not in the AGA 2025 surveillance guideline. That document covers surveillance of untreated Barrett's and states explicitly that eradication therapy "was addressed in a recent AGA guideline". The post-ablation schedules below come from the AGA 2024 endoscopic eradication therapy guideline and from ESGE 2023, and the two do not agree on when — or whether — to stop.
After high-grade dysplasia or intramucosal cancer
AGA 20243 months, 6 months, 12 months, then annually — indefinitely
ESGE 20231, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 10 years, after which surveillance may stop
After low-grade dysplasia
AGA 2024Year 1 and year 3, then revert to non-dysplastic intervals
ESGE 20231, 3 and 5 years, after which surveillance may stop
Where to take the biopsies
AGA 2024Targeted sampling of visible lesions, plus random biopsies of the cardia and the distal 2 cm of tubular oesophagus
ESGE 2023Four-quadrant random biopsies just distal to a normal-appearing neo-squamocolumnar junction
Do not take routine biopsies of normal-looking neosquamous mucosa. ESGE makes this a strong recommendation against. Sample just below the neo-squamocolumnar junction instead — recurrence at the gastro-oesophageal junction is the commonest pattern, and buried glands under intact neosquamous epithelium are not what these biopsies find.
The disagreement that matters: does it ever end? AGA surveils indefinitely after high-grade dysplasia; ESGE stops at 10 years. And the first year differs sharply — AGA does three endoscopies in year one for high-grade dysplasia, ESGE does one. Pick a protocol and apply it consistently rather than blending them.

Chemoprevention and what not to do

PPI at least once daily

Conditional recommendation

Observational evidence suggests around a 71% reduction in progression to high-grade dysplasia or adenocarcinoma. Reasonable in everyone with Barrett's without a contraindication.

Aspirin plus PPI — not recommended

Could not be recommended

Despite favourable AspECT data, the panel cited low event rates, no demonstrated cancer-specific benefit, and insufficient strength of evidence.

Antireflux surgery as an anticancer measure — recommended against

Recommendation against

The baseline progression risk is low, the surgical morbidity is real, and superiority over medical therapy has never been convincingly shown. Operate for reflux symptoms, not to prevent cancer.

When to stop surveillance. Consider stopping when the patient would no longer be a candidate for eradication therapy. AGA 2025 frames it as a conversation rather than a cut-off: begin discussing discontinuation at the earliest potential penultimate endoscopy — for example age 75 in a man without comorbidities. ESGE is more explicit: consider stopping at age 75, and/or where life expectancy is under 5 years, while noting the age cutoff is arbitrary and extension to 80 can be considered. No society sets a mandatory stop age.
What AGA 2025 does not cover. It addresses surveillance only. Screening — who should be tested for Barrett's in the first place — is deferred to a future document, so ACG 2022 remains operative: a single screening endoscopy with chronic reflux plus three or more risk factors from male sex, age over 50, White race, smoking, obesity and a first-degree relative with Barrett's or adenocarcinoma. Note male sex is itself one of the three. A swallowable capsule sponge combined with a biomarker is an acceptable alternative to endoscopy for screening.
Where AGA 2025 declined to rule. It made no recommendation on wide-area transepithelial sampling, on p53 immunostaining, or on TissueCypher — all labelled evidence gaps. It does set guardrails around WATS: it must not substitute for the Seattle protocol, and dysplasia found on WATS alone should not trigger eradication therapy; a discordant result warrants repeat endoscopy in 3 to 6 months. Chromoendoscopy-targeted biopsies likewise do not replace the structured protocol.

Quick answers

Does LA grade A oesophagitis prove GERD?
AnswerNo. Grade A is seen in healthy controls. Lyon 2.0 counts grade B and above as conclusive.
When should the PPI be taken?
Answer30–60 minutes before a meal. Not at bedtime, not with food.
How many times can I switch PPI?
AnswerOnce. More than one switch is not supported — reassess the diagnosis instead.
Who needs indefinite maintenance PPI?
AnswerLA grade C or D — strong recommendation, no withdrawal attempt.
Should I do reflux testing in LA grade C or D?
AnswerNot off therapy. The diagnosis is already conclusive. Test on therapy only if symptoms persist despite optimisation.
Can I diagnose laryngopharyngeal reflux on laryngoscopy?
AnswerNo. The findings correlate poorly with symptoms. Evaluate non-reflux causes first, and test before treating if there is no heartburn.
Is Barrett's under 1 cm still Barrett's?
AnswerNo. A minimum 1 cm segment plus intestinal metaplasia is required.
Should I repeat screening endoscopy if the first was negative?
AnswerNo. The yield is 2–4%.
Low-grade dysplasia — ablate or survey?
AnswerEradication is preferred once expert-confirmed, given roughly 9.1% annual progression. Surveillance at 6 and 12 months then annually is an acceptable alternative.
Does antireflux surgery prevent cancer in Barrett's?
AnswerNo — recommended against for that purpose.

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Adults. A verification note on the AGA 2025 intervals. The published article is paywalled. The numeric intervals attributed to AGA 2025 on this page — the 3-year default, the 5-year extension, the 3 cm cutoff, the 3-month repeat for indefinite-for-dysplasia and the 1 minute per centimetre inspection time — were read from the AGA public-comment draft, cross-checked against the published abstract, which they match wherever the two overlap. The recommendation against surveillance for columnar-lined oesophagus under 1 cm and the strong recommendation for chromoendoscopy are confirmed in the published abstract. Check the interval numbers against the final article before relying on them.
Aspirin: the societies split. ACG could not make a recommendation on aspirin plus PPI. ESGE recommends against aspirin or NSAIDs for chemoprevention outright, at strong strength and moderate quality. AGA 2025 did not address it. Do not present aspirin as a neutral option.
