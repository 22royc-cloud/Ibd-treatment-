Staging without a needle

Baveno VII replaced "compensated cirrhosis" with compensated advanced chronic liver disease — cACLD — because advanced fibrosis and early cirrhosis cannot reliably be separated in practice, and the management is the same. The question that matters is not the histology but whether there is clinically significant portal hypertension.

CSPH — clinically significant portal hypertension

HVPG ≥ 10 mmHg

The threshold above which varices develop, decompensation becomes likely and HCC risk rises. HVPG ≥ 12 mmHg is the threshold for variceal bleeding; ≥ 20 mmHg measured during an acute bleed identifies those who will fail standard treatment and should have a pre-emptive TIPS.

Liver stiffness — the rule of five

Transient elastography, fasted, in a patient without acute hepatitis, cholestasis, congestion or heavy alcohol use in the preceding days — all of which raise stiffness independently of fibrosis.

New in Baveno VII, measured with a dedicated 100 Hz probe. It reflects portal pressure more directly than liver stiffness and is most useful when the liver measurement is equivocal.

Who actually needs a screening endoscopy

Already on a beta blocker for CSPH

No screening endoscopy needed

The most under-used statement in Baveno VII. If the patient is being treated for portal hypertension, the endoscopy result would not change management — so do not do it. Endoscopy is for deciding whether to band, not for confirming what you already know.

Baveno VI criteria — LSM < 20 kPa and platelets > 150

Screening endoscopy can be avoided

Risk of high-risk varices under 5%. Repeat LSM and platelets annually: if the stiffness rises or the platelets fall below the thresholds, do the endoscopy.

Expanded criteria — LSM < 25 kPa and platelets > 110

May avoid endoscopy — less well validated

Increases the proportion spared endoscopy at the cost of a slightly higher miss rate. Reasonable where endoscopic capacity is limited.

After aetiological cure — e.g. HCV SVR, sustained HBV suppression, alcohol abstinence

LSM < 12 kPa and platelets > 150 can leave surveillance

Applies once the cause has been removed and the patient remains compensated. This is a genuine discharge, not a longer interval.

None of the above

Screening endoscopy indicated

The Baveno VII change worth internalising: beta blockers in cACLD are prescribed for CSPH, to prevent decompensation — not for varices, to prevent bleeding. The PREDESCI trial showed carvedilol reduces decompensation, driven mostly by less ascites. The varices are a marker of the pressure, not the target.

Who to treat

cACLD with CSPH, no varices yet

Carvedilol — to prevent decompensation

The central shift in Baveno VII. Following PREDESCI, beta blockers are no longer prescribed only to protect against variceal bleeding — they prevent decompensation, mainly the development of ascites, in anyone with CSPH. The indication is the portal pressure, not the varices.

Small varices with red wale signs, or Child-Pugh C

Non-selective beta blocker

Higher bleeding risk than size alone suggests.

Medium or large varices

Carvedilol preferred · band ligation if beta blockers cannot be used

Baveno VII prefers carvedilol over banding as first-line primary prophylaxis. Reserve EVL for those who cannot take or tolerate a beta blocker — it protects against bleeding but does nothing for portal pressure or for ascites.

No CSPH

No beta blocker

Beta blockers do not prevent varices from forming in the absence of CSPH and simply add side effects.

The drugs

Carvedilol preferred agent in Baveno VII 6.25 mg daily · max 12.5 mg/day
Dose
Start 3.125–6.25 mg once daily, titrate after a few days to 12.5 mg/day — once daily in the trials, though splitting into 6.25 mg twice daily is often better tolerated.
Titration target
Do not chase a heart rate target with carvedilol. Titrate against blood pressure and tolerance. Keep systolic BP above 90 mmHg and MAP above 65 mmHg.
Why this one
Blocks β-receptors and α1-receptors, so it lowers intrahepatic vascular resistance as well as portal inflow. That gives a greater HVPG reduction than propranolol at equivalent β-blockade, and it works in some patients who do not respond to traditional NSBBs.
CautionIts greater blood-pressure effect is the price. Avoid or reduce in refractory ascites with low blood pressure — propranolol may be better tolerated there. Baveno VII advises against carvedilol in decompensated disease with hypotension.
Propranolol traditional non-selective beta blocker 20–40 mg twice daily · max 320 mg/day
Dose
Start 20–40 mg twice daily, titrating every few days. Maximum 320 mg/day, reduced to 160 mg/day if ascites is present.
Titration target
Titrate to the maximum tolerated dose, aiming for a resting heart rate around 55–60 bpm while keeping systolic BP above 90 mmHg.
Why this one
Long track record and cheap. A reasonable choice when carvedilol lowers the blood pressure too far.
CautionHeart-rate response correlates poorly with HVPG response, so a patient at target heart rate is not necessarily protected. Asthma, high-degree AV block and severe peripheral arterial disease are contraindications.
Nadolol once-daily alternative 20–40 mg daily · max 160 mg/day
Dose
Start 20–40 mg once daily. Maximum 160 mg/day, halved to 80 mg/day with ascites.
Titration target
As for propranolol — heart rate 55–60 bpm, systolic BP above 90 mmHg.
Why this one
Once-daily dosing helps adherence. Renally cleared, so it accumulates in renal impairment.
CautionSame class contraindications as propranolol.

When to hold or reduce a beta blocker

Reduce or pause — then restart once resolved
  • Systolic BP below 90 mmHg, or MAP below 65 mmHgRenal perfusion depends on it
  • Acute kidney injury or hepatorenal syndrome
  • Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or any active sepsis
  • Serum sodium below 130 mmol/L
  • Refractory ascites with any of the aboveRefractory ascites alone is not a reason to stop
Pausing is not stopping. The old teaching that beta blockers must be permanently withdrawn in refractory ascites has been softened: hold them during hypotension, AKI or sepsis, and resume once the episode resolves. Permanently abandoning a beta blocker after a single low blood pressure reading loses a protective drug for good.

Acute variceal bleeding

Mortality is around 15–20% at six weeks. Most of what determines the outcome happens in the first few hours, before and around the endoscopy — not at the endoscopy itself.

Minute 0 — before anything else
  • Vasoactive drug immediately, on suspicion, before endoscopy. Terlipressin 2 mg IV every 4 h for the first 48 h then 1 mg every 4 h; or somatostatin 250 µg bolus then 250–500 µg/h; or octreotide 50 µg bolus then 50 µg/h. Continue up to 5 days, and it is reasonable to stop 24–48 h after haemostasis.
  • Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily — antibiotic prophylaxis is not optional. It reduces infection, rebleeding and mortality. Continue up to 7 days.
  • Restrictive transfusion. Threshold haemoglobin 7 g/dL, target 7–9. Transfuse at 8 g/dL if there is cardiovascular disease. Over-transfusion raises portal pressure and provokes rebleeding.
  • Careful crystalloid resuscitation — enough for perfusion, not enough to over-expand.
  • No fresh frozen plasma — it does not correct the coagulopathy of cirrhosis and is associated with worse outcomes. No routine platelet transfusion. INR is not a measure of bleeding risk here and must not delay endoscopy.
Before the scope
  • Erythromycin 250 mg IV 30–120 minutes before endoscopy, to clear the stomach.
  • Airway protection only for ongoing haematemesis, encephalopathy, agitation or a compromised airway — and extubate promptly afterwards. Routine intubation causes pneumonia.
  • Lactulose to prevent encephalopathy.
  • Start a PPI, but as a short course — its role is post-banding ulceration, not the bleeding itself.
Within 12 hours — endoscopy
  • Oesophageal varices: band ligation. Superior to sclerotherapy. Place 5–10 bands, starting at the bleeding point and then spiralling upward from the gastro-oesophageal junction. More than six bands adds nothing.
  • Gastric varices, Sarin classification. GOV2 and IGV1 (cardiofundal): cyanoacrylate injection. GOV1: cyanoacrylate or banding. EUS-guided coil with glue is an option in experienced centres.
  • Record whether there was active bleeding at endoscopy — that single observation decides who gets a pre-emptive TIPS.
Then — decide about TIPS
  • Assess Child-Pugh class and whether bleeding was active at endoscopy. See the TIPS tab.
  • Do not wait to see whether the patient rebleeds. The whole point of pre-emptive TIPS is that it is done before failure, not after.
Terlipressin cautions. Check the sodium — it causes hyponatraemia, sometimes profound and rapid, particularly in younger patients with preserved liver function. Avoid in significant ischaemic heart disease or peripheral vascular disease; watch for skin and bowel ischaemia. Reduce with renal impairment.
Not every upper GI bleed in a cirrhotic patient is variceal. Roughly a third are not. Treat as variceal until the endoscopy says otherwise — the drugs and antibiotics do no harm meanwhile — but do not stop looking once the varices are banded if the picture does not fit.

TIPS and rescue

The decision that most changes survival in this disease is who gets a shunt, and when. Baveno VII is explicit that it should be pre-emptive in high-risk patients rather than a salvage manoeuvre.

Pre-emptive — "early" TIPS

Within 72 hours, ideally within 24

Child-Pugh C with a score of 10–13, or Child-Pugh B above 7 with active bleeding at endoscopy despite vasoactive drugs, or HVPG ≥ 20 mmHg. Done after initial haemostasis, not instead of it. Improves one-year survival and bleeding control without a measurable increase in encephalopathy.

Rescue TIPS

For failure

Failure to control bleeding, or early rebleeding despite drugs and endoscopic therapy. Achieves haemostasis in nearly all cases. The commonest error is leaving it too late, after repeated failed endoscopies.

Self-expanding metal stent

Bridge to definitive therapy

Preferred over balloon tamponade. Provides tamponade for up to 14 days, with about 96% haemostasis and fewer complications.

Balloon tamponade

Temporising only — maximum 24 hours

Controls bleeding in around 90% but causes serious complications in about 20%, including aspiration and oesophageal perforation. Requires a protected airway. A bridge to TIPS or a stent, never a destination.

BRTO

Gastric varices with a gastrorenal shunt

Balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration. Not for oesophageal bleeding. Note it raises portal pressure and can worsen oesophageal varices and ascites.

When TIPS is a poor idea

Relative and absolute limits

Heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, uncontrolled encephalopathy, active uncontrolled sepsis, biliary obstruction, extensive polycystic liver disease. Very high MELD predicts poor outcome — but in the pre-emptive setting the trial populations included sick patients, so do not talk yourself out of it on Child-Pugh score alone.

The recurring failure is treating pre-emptive TIPS as something to consider if things go badly. By then it is rescue TIPS, in a sicker patient, with worse outcomes. Identify Child-Pugh C 10–13 and Child-Pugh B with active bleeding at the time of the index endoscopy, and refer within 72 hours.

After the bleed

Secondary prophylaxis — both, not either

Non-selective beta blocker plus band ligation. The combination is superior to either alone and is the standard of care after a variceal bleed. Prescribing one and omitting the other is the commonest gap in follow-up.

The banding programme

Repeat sessions every 1–4 weeks until varices are eradicated. Then endoscopy at 3–6 months, and every 6–12 months thereafter.

If combination therapy fails

Recurrent bleeding despite an adequate beta blocker and a completed banding programme is an indication for TIPS, and a trigger to consider transplant assessment.

Treat the cause — this outranks everything else

Alcohol abstinence, HBV suppression, HCV cure, weight loss and metabolic control in MASLD. Removing the aetiology lowers portal pressure, can reverse cACLD, and is the only intervention that changes the trajectory rather than managing its consequences.

The rest of the cirrhosis bundle

Six-monthly ultrasound for HCC. Nutrition and sarcopenia assessment. Hepatitis A and B, pneumococcal and influenza vaccination. Avoid NSAIDs and aminoglycosides. Review every drug for hepatic dosing.

Recompensation

A new Baveno VII concept: decompensated cirrhosis is no longer necessarily a one-way street. All three criteria must be met.
Baveno VII recompensation — all three required
  • The cause is removed, cured or suppressedAlcohol abstinence, HBV suppression, HCV cure
  • Resolution of ascites and encephalopathy off diuretics and off prophylactic therapy, and no variceal bleeding for 12 monthsResolution while still on treatment does not count
  • Sustained improvement in liver function — albumin, bilirubin and INR
Recompensated patients still need surveillance. The portal hypertension usually improves rather than disappears, and HCC risk persists.

About this page

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Adults with cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Doses of vasoactive drugs and beta blockers vary between local protocols — check yours. Pre-emptive TIPS requires a centre that can deliver it within the window.
On Baveno VIII. The workshop was held in Baveno in March 2026 but its consensus statements have not yet been published — Baveno VII, published in 2022, remains the operative document. Expect revisions to the non-invasive thresholds and to the role of spleen stiffness when it appears.
