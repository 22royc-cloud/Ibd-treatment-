Two guidelines, shown side by side. USMSTF (ACG, AGA and ASGE together, 2020) and ESGE (2020) genuinely disagree in places — most sharply about small low-risk adenomas, where ESGE removes surveillance entirely. Cards where they differ are marked. Follow whichever your service has adopted, but know that the difference is real and deliberate.

Inadequate bowel preparation
USMSTFRepeat within 1 year
ESGERepeat within 1 year
If the preparation was not adequate to detect lesions > 5 mm, the examination does not count and none of the intervals above apply. Document the Boston score by segment — any segment scoring < 2 is inadequate. Do not simply record the finding-based interval as if the examination had been clean.
Incomplete colonoscopy — caecum not reached
USMSTFRepeat or CT colonography
ESGERepeat or CT colonography
Complete the examination before assigning any interval. A repeat colonoscopy, CT colonography or colon capsule are all reasonable depending on why the first attempt failed.
Resection completeness uncertain
USMSTFEarly site check
ESGEEarly site check
If you are not confident the lesion was completely removed, book a targeted site check rather than a full-interval surveillance. The commonest cause of "interval cancer" at a previous polyp site is incomplete resection, not fast biology.

Average-risk screening

No family history, no IBD, no known syndrome, no symptoms. A symptomatic patient is not being screened — they are being investigated, and none of this applies.

When to start

Age 45

ACG makes this a conditional recommendation at 45–49 and a strong one from 50; the ACS 2026 update and USPSTF both start at 45. Earlier onset disease has been rising for two decades, which is what moved the threshold.

When to stop

Age 75, then individualise

Continue to 75 if life expectancy exceeds 10 years. Between 76 and 85 the decision turns on comorbidity, prior screening history and the patient’s own preference. ACS 2026 recommends against screening beyond 85.

Family history

One first-degree relative with CRC or an advanced polyp diagnosed < 60

Start: Start at age 40, or 10 years before the youngest case — whichever is earlier
Then: Colonoscopy every 5 years

One first-degree relative diagnosed ≥ 60

Start: Start at age 40
Then: Then follow average-risk intervals

Two or more first-degree relatives, any age

Start: Start at age 40, or 10 years before the youngest case
Then: Colonoscopy every 5 years · consider genetic evaluation

Second-degree relatives only

Start: Average-risk timing
Then: Average-risk intervals

These apply to CRC or a documented advanced adenoma in the relative — worth asking about explicitly, since patients rarely volunteer a polyp history. Any suggestion of a syndrome — very early onset, multiple primaries, a cluster of Lynch-spectrum cancers — moves the patient out of this table and into genetic evaluation.

Special situations

IBD — colitis surveillance

First surveillance colonoscopy 8 years after symptom onset (ECCO says 6–8), or immediately at diagnosis of PSC. Extent is defined by the maximum ever documented, endoscopically or histologically — not by what the colon looks like today.

High risk — annual
  • Primary sclerosing cholangitis — from the moment PSC is diagnosed, regardless of colitis extent, activity or duration
  • Colonic stricture within the past 5 years
  • Dysplasia within the past 5 years in a patient who has declined colectomy
  • First-degree relative with CRC diagnosed under 50
  • Extensive colitis with severe active inflammation
Intermediate risk — every 3 years
  • Extensive colitis with mild or moderate active inflammation
  • Post-inflammatory polyps
  • First-degree relative with CRC diagnosed at 50 or older
Low risk — every 5 years
  • Colitis affecting less than half the colon
  • Extensive colitis with no or minimal inflammation
No surveillance needed
  • Proctitis alone
  • No evidence of colonic inflammation
  • Crohn’s disease affecting less than one-third of the colon
Technique. Pan-colonic dye chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies is preferred; high-definition white light with targeted biopsies is an accepted alternative. If neither is available, take 2–4 random biopsies every 10 cm. Surveillance should be done in remission where possible — active inflammation makes dysplasia both harder to see and harder to call.
A note on modern risk. The BSG 2025 guideline puts IBD-associated CRC risk at roughly 1.4–1.7 times the population baseline — far lower than the historical figures that shaped older protocols, largely because inflammation is now better controlled. It moves toward a calculated multivariable risk rather than the single worst risk factor, and allows surveillance to stop after repeated negative examinations without inflammation, or above age 75.

Hereditary syndromes

Lynch syndrome

Colonoscopy every 1–2 years, starting at age 20–25 — or 2–5 years before the earliest CRC in the family, whichever is earlier.

MLH1 and MSH2/EPCAM carriers start at 20–25. For MSH6 and PMS2, where cancers occur later, most guidelines allow starting at 30–35 unless the family history says otherwise. Interval-shortening to annual is reasonable in confirmed carriers.

Classic FAP

Annual colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy from puberty (about age 10–12).

Colectomy is indicated for cancer, significant symptoms, or when multiple adenomas > 6 mm can no longer be adequately controlled endoscopically. Duodenal and ampullary surveillance runs in parallel.

Attenuated FAP

Annual colonoscopy — not sigmoidoscopy — from the late teens.

Polyps are fewer and right-sided, so a sigmoidoscopy misses the disease.

MUTYH-associated polyposis

Annual colonoscopy, managed as for attenuated FAP, from about age 25–30.

Biallelic carriers. Monoallelic carriers have at most a modest risk increase and are generally managed by family history.

Serrated polyposis syndrome

Colonoscopy every 1–3 years, removing all polyps > 5 mm.

WHO criteria — either one: ≥ 5 serrated lesions proximal to the rectum, all ≥ 5 mm, with ≥ 2 that are ≥ 10 mm; or > 20 serrated lesions of any size distributed through the colon, with ≥ 5 proximal to the rectum. Counted cumulatively over multiple examinations. First-degree relatives should be screened. Routine germline testing is not recommended.

When to think of a syndrome: CRC under 50 · more than 10 cumulative adenomas · any serrated polyposis criteria met · a Lynch-spectrum cluster in the family (colorectal, endometrial, ovarian, gastric, small bowel, urothelial, biliary, brain, sebaceous) · deficient mismatch repair or microsatellite instability on tumour testing. Universal MMR/MSI testing of all colorectal cancers is the standard of care.

After resection of colorectal cancer

The clearing examination

A high-quality complete colonoscopy before surgery, or within 6 months after if the tumour was obstructing. Synchronous cancers are found in 3–5% — this examination is not optional.

Then

1 year after resection → 3 years later → 5 years after that. Shorten if high-risk lesions are found along the way.

Endoscopically resected pT1 cancer

Low-risk T1 with clear margins follows the same schedule as any resected CRC. High-risk histology — lymphovascular invasion, poor differentiation, deep submucosal invasion, positive margin — is a surgical discussion, not a surveillance one.

Stop at 80, or earlier if life expectancy is limited by comorbidity. This applies to post-cancer and post-polypectomy surveillance alike.

About this page

Intervals for asymptomatic surveillance and screening in adults.

Clinical decision support, not a prescribing authority. Surveillance intervals are recommendations built on population data; they do not override a specific concern about a specific patient. Verify against whichever guideline your service has formally adopted.
Why two columns. USMSTF and ESGE reach different answers for low-risk findings — most importantly, ESGE returns patients with 1–4 small low-grade adenomas to routine screening with no surveillance at all, while USMSTF books them at 7–10 or 3–5 years. Showing one number would hide a real and deliberate disagreement. Pick the guideline your unit follows and apply it consistently.
The recurring theme in the adherence literature is that surveillance is done too often, not too rarely — particularly for one or two small tubular adenomas. Over-surveillance consumes the capacity needed for the people who actually benefit.
